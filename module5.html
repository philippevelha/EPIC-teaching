0. Light–Matter Interaction in Semiconductors

From the PN junction to light

What we already know (Module 4):

  • Forward bias injects electrons (n side) and holes (p side) into the same region
  • They recombine: an electron drops from the conduction band (CB) to the valence band (VB)
  • The energy \(\approx E_g\) goes either into heat (phonons, traps, Auger) or into a photon

Today’s questions:

  1. Why does recombination produce light in InP/GaAs but almost none in Si?
  2. How can light be amplified instead of just emitted?

Tools we need: the \(E\)–\(k\) diagram, momentum conservation, and Einstein’s three radiative processes.

Recombination rate (carrier density \(N\)):

\[R(N) = \underbrace{A N}_{\text{SRH (traps)}} + \underbrace{B N^2}_{\text{radiative}} + \underbrace{C N^3}_{\text{Auger}}\]

Internal quantum efficiency:

\[\eta_i = \frac{B N^2}{A N + B N^2 + C N^3}\]

\(B\) (cm³/s) \(\eta_i\) typical
GaAs, InGaAsP \(\sim 10^{-10}\) > 50 %
Si \(\sim 10^{-14}\) \(\sim 10^{-6}\)

Four orders of magnitude in \(B\): the reason is in the band structure.

  • Bridge from Module 4: the same forward-biased junction that we used for carrier-injection modulators is the heart of every laser and SOA.
  • The B coefficient is the key number. Keep it in mind: it is directly related to the Einstein A coefficient we will derive later.

The E–k diagram

In a crystal, electrons are Bloch waves:

\[\psi_{n,\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}) = u_{n,\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\, e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\]

  • \(\hbar\mathbf{k}\) = crystal momentum
  • \(E_n(\mathbf{k})\) = band \(n\) (dispersion relation)
  • \(\mathbf{k}\) restricted to the 1st Brillouin zone, \(|k| \lesssim 2\pi/a\)

Curvature = effective mass:

\[\frac{1}{m^*} = \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial k^2}\]

Near an extremum: \(E \approx E_c + \dfrac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m^*}\)

High-symmetry points of the fcc zone: Γ (k = 0), X (along ⟨100⟩), L (along ⟨111⟩), K (along ⟨110⟩).

  • VB maximum is always at Γ for these materials
  • The CB minimum decides everything: Γ (GaAs) → direct; X-side (Si) or L (Ge) → indirect
  • Emphasise that k is a vector: an E–k “diagram” is a cut of a 3D function E(kx, ky, kz) along chosen directions.
  • The model has no spin–orbit coupling, so the gap values are off (real: Si 1.12, Ge 0.66, GaAs 1.42 eV), but the valley positions are right.

Crystal orientation sets the direction of k

The E–k diagram depends on which direction in the crystal we look along: \(\Gamma\to X\) is the [100] direction, \(\Gamma\to L\) is [111].

  • Si: CB minimum at \(k \approx 0.85\,(2\pi/a)\) along ⟨100⟩, not along ⟨111⟩
  • GaAs: minimum at Γ, so it is the same in every direction (isotropic)
  • Along [111] Si has no minimum near the gap; along [100] it does
  • Wrong direction means a different energy, so orientation matters for transport, strain and optics
  • An E–k diagram is a 1D cut of a 3D function; each segment of the band-structure plot is a crystal direction. Γ→X is [100], Γ→L is [111].
  • Si: walking along [100] the conduction band dips to its minimum at 0.85 of the way to X; along [111] it does not. The minimum exists only along the six ⟨100⟩ directions.
  • Practical consequences of direction: effective mass and mobility depend on orientation; strain applied along one axis shifts some valleys and not others; on (001) wafers devices are usually aligned along ⟨110⟩ (the cleavage directions of III-V and the standard wafer flat).
  • Transition to the next slide: instead of cutting E(k) along a few lines, look at it from above, as a map of iso-energy contours.

From orbits to iso-energy roads

Atom: the electron is bound to one nucleus, on discrete orbitals.

Crystal: the electron is shared by all the atoms. It is a Bloch wave labelled by \(\mathbf{k}\), with energy \(E(\mathbf{k})\).

All the states with the same energy form a curve (2D) or a surface (3D) in \(k\)-space: the iso-energy contour. These are the “roads” an electron of energy \(E\) can use.

In a magnetic field the electron really orbits along its road: \(\hbar\,\dot{\mathbf{k}} = -e\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) does no work, so \(E\) stays constant.

Two rules for reading the map:

  1. The velocity is the gradient of the energy: \[\mathbf{v}_g = \frac{1}{\hbar}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{k}} E(\mathbf{k})\] so it is perpendicular to the road, not necessarily parallel to \(\mathbf{k}\).
  2. The speed is large where the contours are close together (steep \(E\)) and small where they spread out.

Free electron: circular roads, \(\mathbf{v} \parallel \mathbf{k}\), the same speed in every direction.

Crystal: the periodic potential bends the roads into squares, then breaks them into disjoint pockets (next slide).

  • Start from the picture students already have: electrons orbiting the nucleus. In a crystal there is no single nucleus to orbit; the electron is spread over the whole lattice and the useful label is k, not a radius.
  • The iso-energy contour plays the role of the orbit: an electron with energy E can only occupy states on that contour. Without collisions or external forces it keeps its k and moves in real space with velocity v_g.
  • The orbit analogy is literally true in a magnetic field: the Lorentz force is perpendicular to v, does no work, so k slides along the iso-energy contour. The real-space trajectory has the same shape as the k-space road, rotated by 90° and scaled by ħ/(eB). This is how Fermi surfaces are measured (cyclotron resonance, de Haas–van Alphen).
  • Group velocity: draw a contour map on the board, like a topographic map. The velocity points uphill, perpendicular to the contour lines, and is fast where the lines are dense.

  • High energy: the road breaks into disjoint pockets around the zone corners. Some directions of \(\mathbf{k}\) have no state at all

The roads in real crystals are not round

The speed depends on the direction:

  • Si CB: 6 disjoint ellipsoids. At 150 meV: \(1.8\times10^5\) m/s along the axis, \(4.9\times10^5\) m/s across (\(m_l = 0.92\), \(m_t = 0.19\,m_0\))
  • Si VB: warped, with heavy and light directions
  • GaAs CB: round and light (\(0.067\,m_0\)), the same in every direction

Why it matters:

  • Unstrained Si is isotropic on average (6 valleys compensate); strain breaks this
  • \(g(E) \propto \oint dS/|\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}E|\): slow directions hold more states, so more carriers are needed for inversion
  • Absorption at \(\hbar\omega\) happens on the road where \(E_c - E_v = \hbar\omega\)
  • These are real band structures (same pseudopotential model as before), cut by the k_z = 0 plane. Compare the Si panel with the free-electron circles of the previous slide: the roads are disjoint and elongated, and they are not centred on Γ.
  • Si numbers: at the same energy, the speed along the long axis of the ellipse is about 2.7 times smaller than across it. Parabolic estimate v = sqrt(2E/m*) with 50 meV: 1.4 vs 3.0 × 10^5 m/s, ratio sqrt(0.92/0.19) ≈ 2.2. At 150 meV non-parabolicity raises the ratio.
  • Ask: an electron in the +k_x valley is pushed by a field along x. Is it heavy or light? (Heavy, m_l.) Now along y? (Light, m_t.) Then why is Si mobility the same along x and y? Because the ±k_y valleys respond the opposite way, and in equilibrium all six valleys are equally filled. The conductivity of a cubic crystal is isotropic; the anisotropy reappears once strain lifts the valley degeneracy.
  • Valence band: without spin–orbit the top of the VB is degenerate at Γ, and the warped “star” shape is the heavy-hole band. Holes moving along ⟨110⟩ and ⟨100⟩ have different masses; this matters for p-type modulators and for hole mobility on different wafer orientations.
  • DOS formula: the number of states in a shell between E and E+dE is the area of the iso-energy surface divided by the speed. Slow directions contribute more. This is why the Si conduction band (6 heavy-ish valleys) has a large effective DOS (2.8 × 10^19 cm^-3) and GaAs a small one (4.7 × 10^17 cm^-3).
  • Bridge to optics: a photon of energy ħω is absorbed by the electrons sitting on the road where the CB and VB are separated by exactly ħω, with the same k (next slides: vertical transitions).

Valleys in k-space

Constant-energy surfaces near the CB minimum:

  • Si: 6 ellipsoids along ±[100], ±[010], ±[001], with an anisotropic mass (\(m_l \neq m_t\))
  • Ge: 8 half-ellipsoids at the L points (⟨111⟩)
  • GaAs: 1 sphere at Γ, light and isotropic

Why it matters in photonics:

  • Strain along a crystal axis lifts the valley degeneracy (strained-Si transistors and modulators)
  • Ge: the direct Γ valley is only ~140 meV above L. Tensile strain plus n-doping pushes Ge towards a direct gap (Ge-on-Si lasers)
  • The photon momentum is ~10⁻³ of the zone size: optical transitions are vertical
  • Explain constant-energy surfaces: the 3D shape of E(k) = constant just above the CB minimum. An ellipsoid means a different effective mass along and across the valley axis.
  • The ±k_z valleys of Si are out of the drawn plane; there are 6 in total. Ge has 8 half-valleys at the zone boundary, equivalent to 4 full ones.
  • Link to Module 4: strain from SiGe or SiN layers splits these valleys, which is used in strained-Si devices. For Ge, the direct Γ valley lies only about 140 meV above L, so tensile strain plus n-doping (filling L states) is a route to Ge-on-Si lasers.
  • Close with the scale argument: the photon wave-vector is about 1/1000 of the distance between valleys, so a photon alone cannot connect them.

Momentum conservation: direct vs. indirect

Energy and momentum must both be conserved:

\(E_2 - E_1 = h\nu\) and \(\mathbf{k}_2 - \mathbf{k}_1 = \mathbf{k}_{ph} \approx 0\)

because \(k_{ph} = 2\pi n/\lambda \approx 1.4\times10^{7}\,\text{m}^{-1}\), while the zone size is \(2\pi/a \approx 1.2\times10^{10}\,\text{m}^{-1}\).

  • Direct gap: CB min and VB max at the same k, so emission is a 1st-order process (fast, ~ns).

  • Indirect gap: a phonon must supply \(\Delta k\). This 2nd-order process is slow (~ms in Si), and non-radiative paths (µs) win.

\(\Rightarrow\) Lasers and SOAs use direct-gap III-V materials, bonded or grown on Si (Section A).

  • Do the numbers on the board: k_photon ≈ 1.4 × 10^7 m^-1 against a zone size of 1.2 × 10^10 m^-1. The photon carries almost no momentum, so an optical transition is a vertical arrow on the E–k diagram.
  • Indirect gap: the electron at the X-side valley needs a phonon to supply Δk. Second-order process, so a much smaller probability; the radiative lifetime becomes ms while defects recombine carriers in µs. The energy goes into heat.
  • The same rule applies to absorption: Si absorbs weakly near its gap (phonon-assisted), which is why Si detectors need tens of µm of material at 1 µm wavelength.
  • Conclusion to state clearly: gain media in Si photonics are always direct-gap III-V materials (or strained, doped Ge in research).

Three radiative processes

Absorption

A photon is destroyed and an electron is promoted VB → CB. This is the photodetector process (Section C).

Spontaneous emission

Happens without any photon present. The emitted light is incoherent in direction, phase and polarisation. It is the origin of LEDs and of the ASE noise in amplifiers.

Stimulated emission

An incoming photon triggers the transition. The new photon is a clone (same \(\mathbf{k}\), frequency, phase and polarisation). This is coherent amplification: lasers and SOAs.

\(N_1, N_2\): populations of the lower and upper levels. \(\rho(\nu)\): spectral energy density of the radiation (J·s/m³).

  • Use the two-level picture with E1 in the VB and E2 in the CB. Each process needs the right occupation: absorption needs a full lower and empty upper state, emission the opposite.
  • Stress the clone property of stimulated emission: same direction, frequency, phase and polarisation. This is why an SOA amplifies a signal coherently, without scrambling the data.
  • Spontaneous emission happens into all modes; only a tiny fraction (the β factor of the rate equations) goes into the waveguide mode. That fraction is what becomes ASE noise.
  • Ask: which process does a photodetector use? A laser? An LED? Which one is unavoidable in an amplifier?

Einstein coefficients

Rate balance for a two-level system in a thermal radiation field:

\[\underbrace{B_{12}\,\rho(\nu)\,N_1}_{\text{absorption}} = \underbrace{A_{21}\,N_2}_{\text{spontaneous}} + \underbrace{B_{21}\,\rho(\nu)\,N_2}_{\text{stimulated}}\]

In thermal equilibrium:

  • Boltzmann: \(\dfrac{N_2}{N_1} = \dfrac{g_2}{g_1}\, e^{-h\nu/k_BT}\)
  • Planck: \(\rho(\nu) = \dfrac{8\pi h \nu^3 n^3}{c^3}\, \dfrac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_BT} - 1}\)

Solving for \(\rho(\nu)\) and requiring agreement with Planck at every temperature:

Einstein relations

\[g_1 B_{12} = g_2 B_{21}\]

\[\frac{A_{21}}{B_{21}} = \frac{8\pi h \nu^3 n^3}{c^3}\]

What they say:

  1. Absorption and stimulated emission are the same process run in opposite directions (same cross-section)
  2. Spontaneous emission is fixed by the stimulated coefficient: a good absorber is a good emitter
  3. \(A_{21} = 1/\tau_{sp}\) grows as \(\nu^3\), so spontaneous emission becomes harder to beat at short wavelengths

The relations are properties of the atom or crystal, so they remain valid out of equilibrium (in a pumped laser).

  • Derivation on the board: write ρ = A21 / (B12 (N1/N2) − B21), substitute Boltzmann, compare term by term with Planck. The T → ∞ limit (ρ → ∞) forces g1B12 = g2B21.
  • Link back: the Einstein A coefficient summed over all photon modes is what sets the radiative B coefficient of the previous slide. Indirect gap means a small matrix element, which means small B12, which means small A21: Si is both a weak absorber near Eg and a weak emitter.

Consequences of the Einstein relations

Stimulated / spontaneous in thermal light:

\[\frac{R_{stim}}{R_{spon}} = \frac{B_{21}\rho}{A_{21}} = \frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1}\]

At 1.55 µm and 300 K this is ~10⁻¹⁴. Light sources at thermal equilibrium (the Sun, bulbs) are spontaneous.

Net stimulated emission (gain) vs. absorption:

\[\frac{R_{stim}}{R_{abs}} = \frac{N_2}{N_1}\quad(g_1=g_2)\]

Gain needs \(N_2 > N_1\), which is population inversion. No temperature gives this (Boltzmann factor < 1), so we must pump: in our case, by current injection in a forward-biased PN junction.

  • Left plot: in thermal light at 1.55 µm and room temperature, stimulated emission is about 10^-14 of spontaneous. Even the Sun (5800 K) is mostly spontaneous in the visible. Stimulated emission dominates only in the far infrared and microwaves (masers came first).
  • Right plot: the Boltzmann factor is always below 1, so heating can never give N2 > N1. Inversion must be created by a non-thermal pump: optical, or current injection in our case.
  • With equal degeneracies, net gain is proportional to N2 − N1: absorption and stimulated emission compete with the same coefficient.

From Einstein coefficients to gain

Photon flux \(\Phi\) travelling along \(z\) through the medium. Each photon can be absorbed (∝ \(N_1\)) or cloned (∝ \(N_2\)):

\[\frac{d\Phi}{dz} = \sigma(\nu)\,(N_2 - N_1)\,\Phi \equiv \gamma(\nu)\,\Phi\]

Transition cross-section (from \(A_{21}\), \(B_{21}\)):

\[\sigma(\nu) = \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi n^2\,\tau_{sp}}\; \mathcal{L}(\nu)\]

(\(\mathcal{L}(\nu)\): normalised lineshape, \(\int \mathcal{L}\, d\nu = 1\))

Three regimes:

Condition \(\gamma(\nu)\) Device
\(N_2 < N_1\) \(< 0\): absorption \(\alpha\) photodetector, EAM
\(N_2 = N_1\) \(0\): transparency —
\(N_2 > N_1\) \(> 0\): gain \(g\) SOA, laser

\[\Phi(L) = \Phi(0)\, e^{\gamma L}\]

Spontaneous emission is not included here. It adds a small, incoherent source term which, once amplified, becomes ASE (amplified spontaneous emission).

  • Derive the flux equation on the board: each photon meets N2 atoms that can clone it and N1 atoms that can absorb it, with the same cross-section σ.
  • The cross-section formula ties gain to the spontaneous lifetime: fast spontaneous emission (direct gap) means large σ. Si, with τ_sp in the ms range, cannot provide useful gain.
  • Transparency (N2 = N1) is an important reference point: it is where the material stops absorbing, and it costs current before any gain appears.
  • The table shows the same material can be a detector, an EAM or an amplifier depending only on the pumping level.

Population inversion in a semiconductor

Under injection, electrons and holes thermalise separately (~100 fs) and get two quasi-Fermi levels \(E_{Fc}\), \(E_{Fv}\). Their separation is set by the forward voltage: \(qV \approx E_{Fc} - E_{Fv}\).

A vertical transition at \(h\nu = E_2 - E_1\):

\[g(h\nu) \propto |M|^2 \rho_r(h\nu)\,\big[f_c(E_2) - f_v(E_1)\big]\]

Stimulated emission ∝ \(f_c(1-f_v)\) and absorption ∝ \(f_v(1-f_c)\). The difference is \(f_c - f_v\).

Bernard–Duraffourg condition

\[E_g < h\nu < E_{Fc} - E_{Fv}\]

  • Below \(E_g\): no states, so no interaction
  • Between \(E_g\) and \(E_{Fc}-E_{Fv}\): gain
  • Above \(E_{Fc}-E_{Fv}\): still absorption
  • Need \(qV > E_g\), so the junction must be driven hard

Because \(m_h \gg m_e\) in InGaAs, the holes stay non-degenerate (\(E_{Fv}\) sits above \(E_v\)). Most of the inversion comes from electron band filling.

  • In a semiconductor there are no isolated levels: E1 and E2 are states in the VB and CB connected by a vertical transition, and their occupation is given by Fermi functions.
  • Under injection the electrons and holes each reach their own equilibrium (quasi-Fermi levels) much faster than they recombine (fs vs. ns), so the Fermi description holds.
  • Derive the condition: net gain ∝ f_c(E2) − f_v(E1) > 0 gives hν < E_Fc − E_Fv. Together with hν > E_g this is the Bernard–Duraffourg condition: the forward voltage must exceed E_g/q.
  • Right plot: in InGaAs the holes are heavy, so their quasi-Fermi level stays above the VB edge. The inversion is mainly provided by the light electrons filling the CB.

Gain spectrum vs. injected carriers

  • Low \(N\): absorption everywhere. Transparency at \(N_{tr}\) ≈ 10¹⁸ cm⁻³
  • The gain band widens and its peak blue-shifts as \(N\) increases (band filling)
  • Gain bandwidth of tens of nm leads to broadband SOAs (30–50 nm) and widely tunable lasers
  • Linearised: \(g(N) \approx a\,(N - N_{tr})\), where \(a = dg/dN\) is the differential gain
  • Quantum wells (step-like DOS) give a larger \(a\), a lower \(N_{tr}\) and a log-shaped \(g(N)\)
  • Modal gain is \(\Gamma g - \alpha\), which links to Section B
  • Walk through the curves from bottom to top: absorption at low N, transparency near 1e18 cm^-3, then a gain band that widens and blue-shifts with N because the bands fill from the bottom.
  • The ▼ markers show E_Fc − E_Fv; above that photon energy the material still absorbs. Below E_g there are no states.

A. Integrated Lasers

  • Silicon cannot provide gain (Section 0), so every integrated laser is about bringing a direct-gap material onto the chip: bonding, growth or external attachment.

Introduction to Integrated Lasers

Challenges in Integration

Why hard to integrate?

  • Direct bandgap required (Si has indirect)
  • High threshold current
  • Poor thermal management
  • Coupling to waveguides
  • Wavelength control

Available platforms:

  • III-V on Si (GaAs, InP)
  • Rare-earth doped waveguides (Er, Yb)
  • Quantum dots
  • Germanium (limited)
  • Hybrid integration

  • Connect the first bullet to Section 0: indirect gap means tiny radiative B coefficient and no useful gain.
  • The periodic table shows where III-V compounds come from: group III (Ga, In, Al) plus group V (As, P, N). Alloys tune the gap and the lattice constant.
  • Mention the main platforms used industrially: heterogeneous III-V/Si (Intel, imec, Tower), flip-chip or micro-transfer printing of finished lasers, and external lasers coupled by fibre.

Laser Fundamentals

Basic Operating Principles

Requirements for lasing:

  1. Optical gain > optical loss
  2. Population inversion (more electrons in excited state)
  3. Optical feedback (cavity, waveguide)
  4. Pumping mechanism (electrical, optical)

Key parameters:

  • Threshold current (\(I_{th}\)): Current needed for gain = loss
  • Quantum efficiency (\(\eta_{QE}\)): Photons per injected carrier
  • Wavelength: Determined by bandgap
  • Linewidth: Spectral width of laser
  • Map the four requirements onto Section 0: gain > loss is γ > α_int + mirror loss; inversion is the Bernard–Duraffourg condition; feedback is the cavity; pumping is the forward-biased junction.
  • Threshold is reached when the round-trip gain equals the round-trip loss; above threshold, the carrier density (and therefore the gain) is clamped and extra current becomes photons.
  • Linewidth matters for coherent links and sensing; semiconductor lasers have broad linewidths (MHz) because of the linewidth enhancement factor α_H, which couples gain and index.

III-V Semiconductors on Silicon

Heterogeneous Integration

Direct bandgap materials:

  • GaAs: 870 nm
  • InGaAs: 1310-1550 nm
  • InP: 920 nm
  • InGaN: 405-520 nm

Bandgap engineering:

  • Ternary alloys (InGaAs)
  • Quaternary (InGaAsP)
  • Strain engineering

  • InGaAs alone has a gap at about 1.65 µm (lattice-matched to InP); InGaAsP quaternaries cover 1.0–1.65 µm at the same lattice constant. This is why InP is the substrate for telecom lasers.
  • Use the composition plot: moving horizontally changes the gap at fixed lattice constant (lattice-matched growth); moving off the line introduces strain, which is useful in thin quantum wells and harmful in thick layers.
  • Strain engineering also changes the valence-band structure (heavy versus light holes), which tunes TE/TM gain and the threshold.

Direct growth challenge

  • Lattice mismatch: InP is 8% larger than Si, GaAs 4%. Beyond a critical thickness the strain relaxes through misfit and threading dislocations (visible in the TEM image).
  • Threading dislocations are non-radiative recombination centres: they kill the A coefficient term and shorten laser lifetime.
  • Other issues: thermal expansion mismatch and anti-phase domains (polar III-V on non-polar Si). Quantum-dot active regions tolerate defects much better, which is why direct growth on Si mostly uses QDs.

Bonding Techniques

Direct and Hybrid Bonding

1. Molecular bonding

  • Surface oxide fusion
  • Precise alignment needed
  • No intermediate layer
  • Strong bond (>90 MPa)

2. Eutectic bonding

  • Metal-based (In, Sn, Au)
  • Lower temperature
  • Thickness variation
  • Easier alignment

Challenge: Thermal stress (CTE mismatch)

  • Bonding avoids the lattice-mismatch problem: the III-V is grown on its native substrate, then bonded upside down onto the patterned SOI and the substrate is removed.
  • Molecular (direct) bonding needs extremely flat, clean surfaces (sub-nm roughness) but gives a thin bonding layer, so the optical mode can couple to the Si waveguide.
  • Adhesive (BCB) bonding is more tolerant but gives a thicker interlayer; eutectic metal bonding is used when the III-V is a finished device that only needs electrical and mechanical contact.
  • Thermal expansion mismatch limits the post-bonding process temperatures (typically below about 300 °C).

Molecular bonding

  • The gain is in the III-V; the wavelength selection is done in Si, here with ring resonators and heaters. This splits the job: III-V for photons, Si for passive filters.
  • Each colour in the spectrum is a different heater power: thermal tuning of the Si filter selects the lasing mode.
  • Ask: why put the filter in Si rather than in the III-V? (Lower loss, high-Q rings, mature fabrication, easy thermal tuning.)

Eutectic bonding

laser bonded through eutectic bonding

  • Eutectic bonding uses a metal alloy (for example AuSn) that melts at a low temperature; it fixes a finished laser chip into a recess (pocket) in the Si wafer.
  • Vertical alignment is set by the etch depth and stand-offs; light is butt-coupled from the laser facet into the Si waveguide, so alignment tolerance is sub-micrometre.
  • Advantage: the laser can be tested before assembly (known-good die). Drawback: serial assembly and coupling loss at the interface.

Pumping Schemes

Electrical vs. Optical

Electrical pumping (preferred):

  • Direct injection into p-i-n structure
  • Continuous wave (CW) operation
  • On-chip integration
  • Lower threshold current with quantum wells (see after)

Optical pumping:

  • External pump laser
  • Flexible wavelength
  • Better for rare-earth ions
  • Typically for hybrid systems
  • Electrical pumping is the only practical option on a chip: the forward-biased PIN junction injects carriers directly into the active region.
  • Optical pumping is used for rare-earth (Er, Yb) waveguide amplifiers and lasers, where the ions are not electrically accessible.
  • Ask: what limits the efficiency of electrical pumping? (Series resistance heating, carrier leakage over the barriers, non-radiative recombination.)

Quantum Well Lasers: Reduced dimensionality

Advantages:

  • Lower threshold current (factor of 10-100) due to:
    • increase DOS and physical proximity of electron and holes (Well thickness: 5-10 nm (quantum confinement))
  • Temperature stability
  • Single longitudinal mode possible
  • High modulation speed

  • Confinement in one direction makes the density of states step-like. Fewer states must be filled to reach transparency, so the threshold falls (link to the gain slides of Section 0).
  • The thin well (5–10 nm) also keeps electrons and holes in the same place, improving overlap and the matrix element.
  • A small confinement factor Γ per well is compensated by stacking several wells (MQW).
  • Strained wells also lift the heavy-/light-hole degeneracy, further lowering the threshold.

Quantum well filling

Typical structure

  • Point out the layers from bottom to top: n-cladding, separate-confinement heterostructure (SCH) for the optical mode, quantum wells for carriers, p-cladding and contact.
  • Two confinements at once: the wells trap carriers (nm scale), the SCH and claddings trap light (µm scale). Γ is the overlap between the two.

Laser Threshold Current

Design Optimization

Threshold current:

\[I_{th} = I_0 + \frac{J_{th} \cdot A}{q \eta}\]

Where:

  • \(J_{th}\) = Transparency current density
  • \(A\) = Junction area
  • \(q\) = Elementary charge
  • \(\eta\) = Internal quantum efficiency

Scaling:

  • Smaller junction → Lower \(I_{th}\) (but harder to handle heat)
  • Quantum wells → 10× reduction
  • Typical: 1-10 mA for ridge waveguide lasers
  • J_th is the threshold current density, not the transparency density: J_th = J_tr + extra current needed for the gain to compensate cavity and mirror losses.
  • Threshold scales with the active area, so shorter and narrower lasers have lower threshold current, but they also have higher mirror loss per unit length and worse heat removal.
  • Typical numbers for heterogeneous III-V/Si DFB lasers: a few mA to tens of mA threshold.

Wavelength Control

Tunable Lasers

1. Temperature tuning

\[\frac{d\lambda}{dT} = 0.3 \text{ nm/K}\]

Simple but slow (ms response)

2. Current tuning

  • Bandgap shrinkage (~0.3 nm/100 mA)
  • Refractive index change
  • Fast (ns response)

3. Distributed feedback (DFB)

  • Bragg grating in cavity
  • Single longitudinal mode
  • Better wavelength stability
  • Thermal drift (≈0.1 nm/K for DFB lasers following the index; ≈0.3–0.5 nm/K for the gain peak) is the dominant issue in WDM systems; it requires temperature control or feedback.
  • Current tuning combines a fast carrier-induced index change (blue shift) with a slower heating effect (red shift); heating usually wins at low frequency.
  • DFB lasers select a single longitudinal mode via a Bragg grating; in Si photonics the grating can be in the Si waveguide under the III-V gain region.

  3. Si modulator (MZM or MRR)

B. Optical Amplifiers

  • Same physics as the laser but without feedback: the gain medium is used single-pass. Everything from Section 0 (inversion, gain spectrum, ASE) applies directly.

Amplifier Fundamentals

Gain coefficient over power P:

\[g = \frac{dP}{dx} \frac{1}{P} = \Gamma g_0 - \alpha\]

Where:

  • \(\Gamma\) = Confinement factor
  • \(g_0\) = Material gain
  • \(\alpha\) = Absorption loss

Gain G:

\[G = e^{g L}\]

L = Amplifier length

  • Distinguish material gain g (from Section 0) and modal gain Γg: only the fraction Γ of the mode that overlaps the active region sees gain.
  • Net gain coefficient Γg − α; the total gain is exponential in length in the small-signal regime.
  • Quick estimate for the board: Γg − α = 30 cm^-1 over 1 mm gives G = e^3 ≈ 20, i.e. 13 dB.

Semiconductor Optical Amplifiers (SOA)

Basic Principles

Structure:

  • p-i-n diode (forward biased)
  • Quantum well active region
  • Waveguide geometry
  • Facet coating (AR/HR)

Operation:

  • Electrical injection
  • Population inversion
  • Stimulated emission
  • Typically 20-30 dB gain

  • An SOA is a laser with the feedback removed: both facets need very low reflectivity (AR coatings, angled facets, tilted waveguides) to avoid gain ripple and lasing.
  • Compare with EDFAs: SOAs are small, electrically pumped and integrable, but have higher noise figure, polarisation dependence and fast gain dynamics that cause crosstalk.
  • The band diagram shows the double heterostructure: carriers are confined to the active region, where the inversion is created.

Amplification Basics

Rate Equations

Carrier density:

\[\frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{I}{qV} - \frac{N}{\tau} - \Gamma v_g g(N) P\]

Photon density:

\[\frac{dP}{dt} = \Gamma v_g g(N) P - \frac{P}{\tau_p} + \beta \frac{N}{\tau}\]

Where:

  • \(I\) = Injection current
  • \(\tau\) = Carrier lifetime
  • \(v_g\) = Group velocity
  • \(\beta\) = Spontaneous emission factor
  • \(\tau_p\) = Photon lifetime
  • Read the carrier equation term by term: injection, spontaneous and non-radiative recombination, and stimulated emission removing carriers (this is the saturation mechanism).
  • Read the photon equation: stimulated gain, loss through the photon lifetime, and the spontaneous emission source term β N/τ (the seed of ASE).
  • In an SOA the photon density varies along z, so in practice these equations are solved along the device, as in the next slide.

How the amplification builds up

Every photon in the guided mode is cloned at a rate \(v_g\,\Gamma g(N)\).

  1. Small signal: \(N\) is pinned by the pump and \(g = g_0\), so the power grows exponentially: a straight line in dB vs. \(z\)
  2. Large signal: stimulated emission consumes carriers faster than the current replaces them. \(N\) drops, \(g\) drops, and the growth slows down near the output
  3. Saturation is local: it starts at the output facet, where the power is highest

Homogeneous saturation model:

\[\frac{dP}{dz} = \left(\frac{\Gamma g_0}{1 + P/P_{sat}} - \alpha\right) P\]

The output saturation power (gain −3 dB) is

\[P_{sat,out} \approx \frac{G_0 \ln 2}{G_0 - 2}\,P_{sat} \approx 0.69\,P_{sat}\]

so a high small-signal gain does not mean a high output power.

  • Left plot: at low input power every curve is a straight line in dB, the signature of exponential gain. At 0 dBm the curve bends early: the device is saturated over most of its length.
  • Physical picture: stimulated emission is a recombination channel. When the signal is strong, it removes carriers faster than the current can supply them, so N and therefore g decrease.
  • Right plot: the output saturation power (−3 dB point) is a specification found on every SOA datasheet; typical values are 10–20 dBm.
  • Ask: how would you increase P_sat? (Larger mode area, lower differential gain, shorter carrier lifetime, or a tapered waveguide.)

Gain-Bandwidth Product

Fundamental Trade-off

GBP (Gain × Bandwidth):

\[GBP = g_0 v_g \tau = constant\]

Implication

  • High gain → narrow bandwidth
  • Lower gain → wider bandwidth
  • Typical: GBP ~ 1-2 THz for SOA

Example:

  • 20 dB gain (100×) @ 10 GHz
  • 10 dB gain (10×) @ 100 GHz
  • Caution: a fixed gain-bandwidth product applies to resonant (Fabry–Pérot) amplifiers, where higher gain sharpens the resonances. A travelling-wave SOA has an optical bandwidth set by the gain spectrum (30–50 nm), which does not shrink with gain in the same way.
  • For data signals, the relevant speed limit is the carrier lifetime (gain recovery, ~100 ps), which causes pattern effects in saturation.
  • Use this slide to discuss residual facet reflectivity: even 10^-4 reflectivity creates gain ripple at high gain.

Noise Figure

Fundamental Noise Sources

Amplified spontaneous emission (ASE):

\[P_{ASE} = 2 (G-1) h\nu \Delta\nu\]

Where:

  • \(h\nu\) = Photon energy
  • \(\Delta\nu\) = Bandwidth
  • Factor of 2 from both polarizations

Noise figure:

\[NF = \frac{SNR_{in}}{SNR_{out}} = 1 + \frac{P_{ASE}}{G P_{in}}\]

Minimum NF:

\[NF_{min} = \frac{2(G-1)}{G} \approx 2 \;(= 3\text{ dB, for } G \gg 1)\]

  • Noise figure compares SNR at the input and output of the amplifier, assuming a shot-noise-limited input.
  • ASE power is proportional to G − 1, the photon energy and the optical bandwidth. An optical filter after the SOA reduces the ASE reaching the receiver.
  • The quantum limit is NF = 2 (3 dB) for an ideal, fully inverted amplifier; the next slide shows where that comes from.

ASE: spontaneous emission comes back

Spontaneous photons emitted into the guided mode are amplified like the signal. The result is amplified spontaneous emission (ASE), a broadband incoherent background.

From the Einstein relations, the ratio of spontaneous emission into one mode to net stimulated emission is the inversion factor:

\[n_{sp} = \frac{f_c(1-f_v)}{f_c - f_v} = \frac{1}{1 - e^{(h\nu - \Delta E_F)/k_BT}} \;\geq 1\]

ASE power per polarisation in a bandwidth \(\Delta\nu\):

\[P_{ASE} = n_{sp}\,(G-1)\,h\nu\,\Delta\nu\]

Noise figure (signal–spontaneous beat):

\[NF \approx 2\,n_{sp}\,\frac{G-1}{G} \;\xrightarrow{G \gg 1}\; 2\,n_{sp}\]

  • Perfect inversion (\(n_{sp}=1\)) gives the quantum limit NF = 3 dB
  • Incomplete inversion near the gain edge (\(h\nu \to \Delta E_F\)) makes \(n_{sp} \to \infty\)
  • Input coupling loss adds directly to the NF (dB for dB)

The best NF comes from operating where \(h\nu \ll \Delta E_F\), i.e. high current and the long-wavelength side of the gain peak.

  • n_sp measures how complete the inversion is: n_sp = 1 when the lower state is empty (f_v = 0); it diverges at the transparency edge where f_c ≈ f_v.
  • This is the Einstein A coefficient reappearing: the same matrix element that gives gain also gives spontaneous emission into the guided mode.
  • The 3 dB quantum limit is fundamental for phase-insensitive amplifiers; it cannot be beaten by better engineering.
  • Coupling loss at the input acts before any gain, so it adds dB for dB to the noise figure; this is why pre-amplifiers benefit from being integrated on the same chip as the source.

Typical curves

  • Noise figure versus wavelength: NF is lowest where inversion is strongest; it rises at the gain edges where n_sp grows.
  • ASE spectrum: it has the shape of the gain spectrum. Ask students to read the gain bandwidth off the ASE plot; it is a simple way to characterise an SOA without a tunable source.

Saturation and linear regime

  • Linear regime: gain is independent of input power, so the signal is amplified without distortion.
  • Saturated regime: the gain depends on the instantaneous power, causing pattern effects, cross-gain modulation between WDM channels and chirp. These are drawbacks for amplification but are used deliberately for wavelength conversion and all-optical logic.

Noise Figure Trade-offs

Design Optimization

Lower NF requires:

  • High gain (minimize ASE relative to signal)
  • Low spontaneous emission factor
  • Longer amplifier (if material limited)
  • Higher current density (carefully)

Practical NF values:

  • State-of-art: 4-5 dB @ 20dB gain
  • Typical: 5-9 dB
  • Limited by: ASE, background current, mode overlap
  • Higher current improves inversion (lower n_sp) but heats the device and red-shifts the gain.
  • Input coupling loss adds directly to the NF: a 3 dB fibre-to-chip loss before the SOA adds 3 dB to its noise figure. This is a key argument for on-chip integration.
  • Practical SOA noise figures (5–9 dB) are well above the 3 dB limit because of incomplete inversion, internal loss and coupling.

Saturation and Power Handling

Saturation power:

\[P_{sat} = \frac{h\nu\, A_{mode}}{a\, \tau}, \qquad A_{mode} = \frac{w\,d}{\Gamma}\]

(\(a = dg/dN\): differential gain, \(\tau\): carrier lifetime)

Gain compression:

\[g(P) = \frac{g_0}{1 + P/P_{sat}}\]

Consequences:

  • Maximum output power: 10-20 dBm
  • Output power limits gain achievable
  • Important for cascaded amplifiers
  • P_sat depends on the mode area, the differential gain a and the carrier lifetime: a larger mode or a faster lifetime increases saturation power. This is why booster SOAs use wide or tapered waveguides.
  • The saturation law applies to the local material gain g, not to the total gain G; the total gain compression follows from integrating along the device (previous slides).
  • Booster amplifiers operate near saturation; pre-amplifiers stay in the linear regime.

Spectral Gain Characteristics

Characteristics:

  • Peak gain at specific wavelength
  • Bandwidth: 30-50 nm typical
  • Temperature sensitive (~0.5 nm/K)
  • Gain ripple (±3 dB) (dynamics)

Wavelength range:

  • O-band SOA: 1260-1360 nm
  • C-band SOA: 1530-1565 nm
  • L-band SOA: 1570-1625 nm
  • Link to Section 0: the gain spectrum widens and blue-shifts with carrier density (band filling). The plot shows this measured on a real device.
  • The gain peak red-shifts with temperature (about 0.5 nm/K) because the bandgap shrinks; at high current this partly cancels the band-filling blue shift.
  • Choose the active material (QW composition) so that the gain peak is at the band of interest (O, C or L).

C. Photodetectors

  • Photodetection is the absorption process of Section 0, with the generated carriers swept out by a reverse-biased junction.

Photodetection Principles

Absorption to Current

Photocurrent:

\[I = R \cdot P\]

Where:

  • \(R\) = Responsivity (A/W)
  • \(P\) = Optical power (W)

Quantum efficiency:

\[\eta = \frac{\text{electrons collected}}{\text{photons incident}} = \frac{R h\nu}{q}\]

Typical: 0.5-0.9 (50-90%)

  • Responsivity and quantum efficiency are the same information: R = η q/(hν). At 1550 nm the maximum possible R is about 1.25 A/W (η = 1).
  • Typical Ge waveguide photodiodes reach 0.8–1.1 A/W at 1310–1550 nm.
  • Point out on the figure: carriers generated in the depleted region drift quickly; those generated outside must diffuse, which is slow and gives a long tail in the impulse response.

Photodetector Requirements

Specifications for Integrated Systems

  1. High responsivity (>0.8 A/W)
  2. Low dark current (<1 nA)
  3. High speed (>25 GHz)
  4. Low noise (< -150 dBm/Hz)
  5. Integrated on Si (for photonics)
  6. Low capacitance (match to transimpedance amp)
  • These specifications are coupled: higher speed needs a thinner absorber and smaller capacitance, which tends to reduce responsivity. This trade-off returns several times in the section.
  • Dark current matters for low-power links and for sensing; for high-speed datacom the TIA thermal noise usually dominates.

Absorption of material

  • Link to the E–k diagrams: Si absorption at 1 µm is weak because it is indirect; Ge absorbs strongly up to about 1.55 µm because its direct gap at Γ (0.80 eV) is only slightly above the indirect one.

Silicon Photodetectors

Band-to-Band Absorption

Wavelength limit for Si:

\[\lambda_{cutoff} = \frac{hc}{E_g} = \frac{1240 \text{ nm·eV}}{1.12 \text{ eV}} \approx 1100 \text{ nm}\]

  • Integrable on SOI

Challenges:

  • Lattice mismatch: 4.2%
  • Defect-rich initially
  • Dark current relatively high
  • Requires careful growth
  • Thermal issues (Ge heats up)
  • The 0.67 eV gap is indirect; the useful absorption at telecom wavelengths comes from the direct gap at 0.80 eV (about 1550 nm). Absorption falls steeply beyond about 1600 nm, so the practical cutoff is closer to 1600 nm than to 1850 nm.
  • Ge grown on Si is under small tensile strain (from thermal expansion mismatch), which shrinks the direct gap and extends detection into the L-band.
  • The 4.2% lattice mismatch produces threading dislocations, which are the main source of dark current.

Ge Integration on Si

Epitaxial Growth

Growth techniques:

  1. CVD (Chemical Vapor Deposition)
    • Selective or non-selective
    • Growth temperature: 350-500°C
    • Quality depends on precursor
  2. MBE (Molecular Beam Epitaxy)
    • More controlled
    • Lower defect density
    • More expensive
  • Ge is grown selectively in oxide windows on the Si waveguide, typically with a low-temperature seed layer followed by high-temperature growth and annealing to reduce defect density.
  • CVD is the industrial method (it is compatible with CMOS lines); MBE is mainly used for research.
  • Thermal budget: Ge is grown late in the process because it cannot withstand the high-temperature steps used for Si.

PIN Photodiode Structure

Architecture for High Speed

  • Intrinsic width: 0.5-1.5 μm
  • Optimizes speed vs. responsivity
  • The intrinsic region is where the field is high: carriers generated there drift at saturation velocity, giving a fast response.
  • Doping can be in the Si below the Ge (lateral or vertical PIN), avoiding implants and contacts directly on the Ge, which reduces dark current and contact resistance.

Photodiode Speed Analysis

Bandwidth Limitations

1. Transit time:

\[\tau_{transit} = \frac{W}{v_{sat}}\]

Where:

  • \(W\) = Intrinsic width
  • \(v_{sat}\) = Saturation velocity (\(~10^7 cm/s\) for Ge)
  • Result: 5-10 ps for 0.5-1 μm

2. RC time constant:

\[\tau_{RC} = R_{load} C_{junction}\]

Where:

  • \(R_{load}\) = Transimpedance input impedance (50 Ω)
  • \(C_{junction}\) = Reverse-bias capacitance (10-100 fF)
  • Result: 5-20 ps

3-dB bandwidth:

\(f_{3dB} = \frac{0.44}{\tau_{total}} (for combined effects)\)

  • Two independent limits: transit time across the depletion region, and RC charging of the junction capacitance through the load.
  • Transit-limited bandwidth is about 0.45 v_sat / W; RC bandwidth is 1/(2πRC). They combine approximately as 1/f² = 1/f_tr² + 1/f_RC².
  • Thinner W speeds up transit but increases capacitance (C = εA/W): there is an optimum, which the project explores.

Waveguide-Integrated Photodetector

Evanescent Coupling Design

Advantages over edge-coupling:

  • All photons absorbed in waveguide
  • Can achieve >95% efficiency
  • Compact integration
  • Good alignment tolerance
  • Key idea: in a waveguide detector light propagates along the absorber, so the absorption length (µm along the waveguide) is decoupled from the drift distance (sub-µm across the junction).
  • This breaks the efficiency–bandwidth trade-off of surface-normal photodiodes (see the bandwidth-efficiency slide).
  • Evanescent coupling from Si into Ge happens because Ge has a higher index (about 4.2 vs 3.48).

Photodetector Responsivity

Optimization Strategies

Absorption depth vs. drift:

  • Longer intrinsic region → more absorption
  • But slower drift/diffusion
  • Optimal: ~1 μm for 1550 nm

Efficiency loss mechanisms:

  • Reflection: Use anti-reflective coating (-5%)
  • Recombination: Use back-contact (-2%)
  • Collection: Near bandgap (-1%)
  • Typical total: 85-95%
  • In a surface-normal detector the absorber thickness sets both efficiency and transit time; in a waveguide detector the device length sets efficiency instead.
  • The listed loss mechanisms are illustrative; in waveguide PDs the main losses are usually the absorption in metal contacts and incomplete absorption at the long-wavelength edge.

Dark Current and Noise

Sources of Dark Current

1. Diffusion current (junction edges):

\[I_{diff} \propto \sqrt{T^3} e^{-E_g/kT}\]

Doubles every 5-6°C

2. Generation-recombination (depletion region):

\[I_{gen} \propto \sqrt{T} e^{-E_g/2kT}\]

More temperature sensitive

Thermal management required!

  • Diffusion current scales as n_i², i.e. as T³ exp(−E_g/kT); generation current in the depletion region scales as n_i, i.e. as T^(3/2) exp(−E_g/2kT). The diffusion component is therefore the more temperature-sensitive one.
  • In Ge-on-Si detectors, generation at threading dislocations and at surfaces usually dominates the dark current.
  • Dark current adds shot noise: important for sensing and low-power links, less so for high-speed receivers dominated by TIA noise.

Avalanche Photodetectors

Impact Ionization Principle:

  • High reverse bias (~10-100 V for Ge)
  • Carriers gain energy → create electron-hole pairs
  • Avalanche multiplication
  • Gain: M = 5-100

Figure of merit:

\[M = \frac{I_{out}}{I_{in}}\]

Noise factor:

\[F = k M + (1-k)(2-\frac{1}{M})\]

Where k = ionization coefficient ratio

  • In a high field, carriers gain enough energy to create new electron-hole pairs (impact ionisation), giving internal gain M.
  • The excess noise factor F depends on k, the ratio of hole to electron ionisation coefficients. Si has a very low k (about 0.02–0.05), so Ge/Si APDs use Ge for absorption and Si for multiplication (SACM structure).
  • Gain is useful when the receiver is limited by thermal (TIA) noise, not shot noise.

Performance Comparison

Parameter PIN APD

Selection: PIN for high-speed, APD for sensitivity

  • APDs have a gain-bandwidth product (the avalanche build-up takes time), so high gain reduces bandwidth.
  • Ge/Si APDs operate at lower bias (tens of V or less) than the 100 V+ of older III-V APDs.
  • Rule of thumb: PIN for short-reach high-speed links, APD for sensitivity-limited links (long reach, PON, LiDAR).

Transimpedance Amplifier (TIA)

Receiver Front-End

Key function:

  • Convert photocurrent to voltage
  • Amplify weak signals
  • Minimize noise

Transfer impedance:

\[Z(f) = \frac{V_{out}}{I_{in}}\]

Typically: 10-100 kΩ

  • The TIA converts the photocurrent into a voltage while presenting a low input impedance to the photodiode, which keeps the photodiode RC time short.
  • Its feedback resistor sets the gain and is also the main source of thermal noise, so the TIA usually limits receiver sensitivity in PIN receivers.

TIA Design Considerations

Circuit Implementation

Transimpedance value:

  • Higher Z → More gain, but higher noise and lower bandwidth
  • Lower Z → Higher bandwidth, but needs more gain
  • Typical trade-off: 50-100 kΩ

Feedback network:

Resistor feedback (transimpedance):

  • Dominant pole: \(f_p = \frac{1}{2\pi Z C_{in}}\)
  • High linearity
  • Thermal noise

Capacitor feedback (transimpedance):

  • Inverting integrator-type
  • Better bandwidth
  • Lower thermal noise
  • More complex stabilization
  • The photodiode capacitance appears at the TIA input; the closed-loop bandwidth is roughly the open-loop amplifier gain divided by 2πR_F C_in. Lower photodiode capacitance therefore allows higher R_F and lower noise.
  • This is why co-design of the photodiode and the TIA (and short connections, ideally 3D or monolithic integration) matters.

Receiver Sensitivity

System-Level Performance

Noise contributions:

\[I_{noise}^2 = I_{dark}^2 + I_{shot}^2 + I_{thermal}^2\]

Shot noise:

\[I_{shot} = \sqrt{2qI_{signal}B}\]

Thermal noise:

\[I_{thermal} = \sqrt{\frac{4kT B}{Z}}\]

Receiver sensitivity:

Typically @ \(BER = 10^{-12}\):

  • PIN receiver: -20 to -15 dBm
  • APD receiver: -30 to -25 dBm
  • The noise terms add as variances (powers): dark-current shot noise, signal shot noise and thermal noise.
  • For PIN receivers thermal noise dominates; an APD multiplies the signal above it, which explains the 10 dB sensitivity advantage.
  • Sensitivity is defined at a target bit error rate; with forward error correction modern links run at BER of about 10^-3 to 10^-4 before correction.

High-Speed Photodetector Design

50+ GHz Requirements

Design trade-offs:

  1. Ultra-thin intrinsic region (0.3-0.5 μm)
    • Faster transit
    • Lower capacitance
    • Trade-off: Lower responsivity?
  2. Optimized mesa size
    • Smaller → Lower C
    • Must avoid leakage current
  3. Careful contact design
    • Minimize parasitic resistance
    • Low-loss contacts
  • Bring together transit time, RC, and parasitics: at 50+ GHz the pad and interconnect capacitance can be as large as the junction capacitance.
  • In waveguide PDs, reducing the intrinsic width does not cost responsivity (the length sets absorption), so the question mark on the trade-off is mainly for surface-normal detectors.
  • State-of-the-art Ge waveguide PDs exceed 100 GHz bandwidth with about 0.5–1 A/W.

Bandwidth-Efficiency Product

Fundamental Limit

Responsivity-bandwidth trade-off:

\[R \cdot BW = \text{constant}\]

(approximately, for given design)

Example:

  • 25 GHz @ 1 A/W
  • 50 GHz @ 0.5 A/W

Optimization: Choose based on system link budget

  • This constant product holds for surface-normal (vertically illuminated) photodiodes, where absorber thickness sets both quantities.
  • Waveguide photodetectors and travelling-wave designs largely escape this limit; ask students to explain why from the previous slides.

Memory Types and Applications

Use Cases in Photonics

  • Reversible crystalline-amorphous transition

  • Used in: Neuromorphic, storage

2. 2D materials

Phase-Change Materials (PCM)

Reversible Phase Transition

Material: Ge₂Sb₂Te₅ (GST)

States:

  • Crystalline: Metallic, low bandgap (~0.6 eV)
    • High absorption
    • Rapid thermal decay
  • Amorphous: Semiconductor, high bandgap (~2 eV)
    • Low absorption
    • Good optical transparency
  • GST switches between an amorphous and a crystalline phase with very different optical constants; the state is retained without power.
  • Check the numbers: the optical gap of amorphous GST is about 0.7 eV and crystalline about 0.5 eV, and crystalline GST is strongly absorbing at 1550 nm. Low-loss alternatives (Sb2Se3, Sb2S3, GSST) are used when absorption must be avoided.
  • Switching is thermal: amorphisation needs melting and fast quenching (ns), crystallisation needs heating above the crystallisation temperature for longer (100 ns–µs).

PCM-Based Photonic Devices

Tunable Waveguide Resonators

Concept:

  1. PCM deposited on/near waveguide
  2. Apply thermal pulse (electrical heating)
  3. Phase transition changes optical properties
  4. Resonance wavelength shifts

Modulation depth:

\[\Delta n = n_{cryst} - n_{amorp} \approx 1-2\]

Refractive index modulation: Huge! (much larger than carrier injection)

  • The index change between phases (Δn ≈ 1–3) is orders of magnitude larger than plasma dispersion (Δn ≈ 10^-3) from Module 4, so devices can be µm long.
  • The cost is absorption (Δk) in the crystalline phase; GST devices are often used as amplitude modulators or memory cells rather than pure phase shifters.
  • Heating can be done optically (pulses through the waveguide) or electrically (doped-Si, ITO or graphene micro-heaters).

Graphene Integration

2D Material Properties

Graphene characteristics:

  • Single atomic layer
  • Linear band structure (zero bandgap)
  • Broadband absorption (2.3%)
  • Tunable Fermi level
  • High carrier mobility

Integration methods:

  • Transfer onto Si waveguide
  • CVD growth
  • Oxide-mediated adhesion
  • Graphene absorbs πα ≈ 2.3% per layer at normal incidence, independent of wavelength; in a waveguide the interaction length makes this large.
  • Gating moves the Fermi level: when 2|E_F| exceeds the photon energy, interband transitions are Pauli-blocked and graphene becomes transparent. This is the modulation mechanism.
  • Main practical difficulty: wafer-scale transfer of clean, high-mobility graphene.

Graphene-Based Devices

Electro-optic modulation:

\[\alpha \propto \frac{\text{number of charge carriers}}{E_F}\]

Where \(E_F\) = Fermi level (controlled by gate voltage)

Advantages:

  • Broadband (1-40 μm)
  • Fast tuning (ps)
  • Compact devices
  • Ultra-thin (atomic scale)

Challenges:

  • Low optical interaction per layer
  • Multiple layers needed
  • Integration complexity
  • Clarify the formula: modulation comes from Pauli blocking (absorption switches off when 2|E_F| > ħω), not from absorption proportional to carrier number.
  • Bandwidth is limited by the RC of the gate capacitor and contact resistance, not by intrinsic carrier dynamics (ps).
  • Graphene photodetectors (photo-thermoelectric, bolometric) are also very fast, but responsivity is lower than Ge.

E. Isolators and Non-Reciprocal Devices

  • Every passive structure made of ordinary materials is reciprocal (Lorentz reciprocity). To make an isolator we must break time-reversal symmetry: magnetic field, time modulation or nonlinearity.

Non-Reciprocal Media

Faraday Effect

Magneto-optic rotation:

\[\theta = V B L\]

Where:

  • \(V\) = Verdet constant (rad/T/m)
  • \(B\) = Magnetic field
  • \(L\) = Propagation length

In silicon:

\[V \approx 0.3 \text{ rad/T/m} \text{ (at 1550 nm)}\]

Problem: Silicon Verdet constant too small!

  • Contrast Faraday rotation with optical activity: in Faraday rotation the rotation direction is set by the magnetic field, not by the propagation direction, so light that goes back and forth accumulates twice the rotation instead of cancelling it.
  • Silicon has a very small magneto-optic response; magnetic garnets (Ce:YIG, Bi:YIG) are orders of magnitude stronger and are the standard choice.

Integrated Isolators

Design Approaches

1. With rare-earth dopants:

  • Er-doped Si: Enhanced Verdet constant
  • Requires: Thermal control, permanent magnet

2. With magnetic materials:

  • Iron garnet (TIG) bonded to Si
  • High Verdet constant
  • Hybrid integration

3. With 2D materials:

  • Graphene with magnetic proximity effect
  • Emerging research
  • The established approach bonds or deposits Ce:YIG on Si and uses a non-reciprocal phase shift in an MZI or ring, usually for the TM mode.
  • Magnet-free alternatives: spatio-temporal modulation (travelling-wave modulators), and nonlinear isolators (limited by dynamic reciprocity).
  • Rare-earth doped Si and graphene are research topics, not production solutions.

Isolator Application: Laser Protection

Use in Photonic Systems

Problem: Reflected light back into laser

Consequences:

  • Injection current noise
  • Wavelength drift
  • Reduced linewidth
  • System instability

Solution: Isolator in laser output

Isolation: 30-50 dB typical

  • Reflections back into a laser cavity cause intensity noise, mode hopping and linewidth broadening (coherence collapse), not a reduced linewidth. Very weak, controlled feedback can narrow the linewidth, but uncontrolled reflections degrade it.
  • On-chip reflections from couplers and grating couplers are a real problem for heterogeneous lasers; quantum-dot lasers are much more tolerant of feedback, which is one reason they are attractive without isolators.

Amplifier Design Exercise

Performance Optimization

Scenario: Design SOA for use as pre-amplifier

Specifications:

  • Gain: 25 dB
  • Noise figure: <6 dB
  • Bandwidth: >10 GHz
  • Wavelength: 1550 nm

Tasks:

  1. Calculate required length (use \(g_0\) and \(\alpha\) data)
  2. Estimate gain vs. current
  3. Analyze noise contribution
  4. Optimize for given specifications
  5. Compare with commercial devices
  • The specifications are compatible with the slides: 25 dB gain needs ln(316) ≈ 5.8 of net gain length product.
  • Students should check the saturation output power and the noise figure against the input power range of the application (pre-amplifier: very low input).

Step 1: Length Calculation

  • Use the equation:

    \(G = exp^{ (g_0 - \alpha) L}\) where:

    • \(G\): Gain (linear)
    • \(g_0\): Modal gain coefficient
    • \(\alpha\): Internal loss
    • \(L\): Length

  • Rearranged:

    \(L = \frac{\ln(G)}{g_0 - \alpha}\)

  • Plug in device-specific \(g_0\), \(\alpha\) values.

  • Use the modal gain Γg_0 in the formula (the slide writes g_0 for the modal gain coefficient).
  • Example: Γg_0 − α = 60 cm^-1 gives L = 5.8/60 cm ≈ 1 mm for 25 dB.

Step 2: Gain vs Current

  • SOA gain increases with bias current before saturation.
  • Example curve:
    • \(35~\textrm{dB}\) gain @ \(1.4~\textrm{A}\)
    • Commercial devices: \(13-16~\textrm{dB}\) @ \(350-600~\textrm{mA}\)
  • Target: Use an operating point before gain saturation for optimal noise.
  • Gain increases with current until heating and ASE self-saturation cause it to roll over.
  • Relate to Section 0: higher current means higher carrier density, higher material gain, and a blue-shifted gain peak.

Step 3: Noise Analysis

  • Noise Figure (NF): Dominated by ASE.
    • \(NF \approx 5-8~\textrm{dB}\)
  • Factors:
    • Carrier density
    • Drive current
    • Waveguide & quantum well design
    • Input/output power (avoid saturation)
  • Use NF ≈ 2 n_sp plus input coupling loss; check how much of the noise figure budget is consumed by the fibre-to-chip interface.

Step 4: Optimization

  • Key levers:
    • Quantum well material and structure
    • Waveguide loss minimization
    • Bias current (operate below saturation)
    • AR coatings, polarization sensitivity
  • Goal: Balance gain, noise & bandwidth for required specs.
  • Polarisation sensitivity is often overlooked: QW SOAs favour TE unless strain is engineered to balance TE and TM gain.
  • Long SOAs give more gain but also more ASE and saturation by ASE at the output end.

Advantages:

  • Temperature insensitive
  • Low threshold current
  • Narrow linewidth
  • Long wavelength tunable (1.3-1.55 μm possible)

Challenges:

  • Integration with Si
  • Thermal tuning limitations
  • Growth complexity

Status: Research phase, promising for future integrated sources

  • Quantum dots confine carriers in 3D, giving delta-like density of states: low threshold, low temperature sensitivity and tolerance to defects.
  • That defect tolerance is why QD lasers grown directly on Si have reached long lifetimes; they are in commercial development, not only research.
  • QD lasers are also much less sensitive to optical feedback, which relaxes the need for isolators.

Emerging Integration Platforms

Beyond Silicon Photonics

Alternative platforms:

Silicon Nitride (SiN):

  • Lower loss (0.1 dB/cm)
  • Lower nonlinearity
  • Larger mode area
  • Good for filters, resonators

Lithium Niobate (LiNbO₃):

  • High-speed modulation
  • Strong Pockels effect
  • Recently thin-film LiNbO₃
  • On-Si hybrid possible

III-V Semiconductors (GaAs/InP):

  • Native lasers
  • Direct detection possible
  • Smaller devices (shorter wavelength)
  • Integration with electronics challenging

Polymer Photonics:

  • Low cost
  • Fast modulators
  • Thermal tuning
  • Less stable long-term
  • Each platform fills a gap of Si: SiN for low loss and visible light, thin-film LiNbO3 for Pockels modulation, InP for monolithic lasers and amplifiers.
  • Heterogeneous integration combines several platforms on one chip, which is the current industry direction.

Part 1: SOA Fundamentals

  • Recap of the SOA model with time dependence added.

What is a Semiconductor Optical Amplifier?

Key Features:

  • Active device that amplifies optical signals
  • Based on stimulated emission in semiconductors
  • Carrier density determines gain
  • Nonlinear response tied to carrier lifetime

Main Challenge:

Slow gain recovery (~100 ps) compared to fiber nonlinearities (~fs)

  • The main limitation for signal processing is the slow gain recovery (carrier lifetime ~100 ps), which causes pattern effects at high bit rates.
  • Compare with fibre nonlinearities (Kerr, fs response but weak): SOA nonlinearities are strong but slow.

Physical Mechanisms

Three Key Processes:

  1. Gain Saturation
    • Input signal depletes carrier density
    • Reduces available gain for trailing portions
  2. Self-Phase Modulation (SPM)
    • Intensity-dependent refractive index changes
    • Creates frequency chirp on pulses
  3. Amplified Spontaneous Emission (ASE)
    • Spontaneous emission gets amplified
    • Can saturate the gain at high currents
  • Gain saturation and SPM are linked: when carriers are depleted, both the gain and the refractive index change (the coupling is the linewidth enhancement factor α).
  • ASE acts as an extra, always-present saturating signal; that is the key to the next part.

Mathematical Description

Signal Evolution:

\[\frac{\partial A}{\partial z} + \frac{1}{v_g}\frac{\partial A}{\partial t} = \frac{g}{2}(1-i\alpha)A\]

Where:

  • \(A(z,t)\) = complex signal amplitude
  • \(g\) = optical gain
  • \(\alpha\) = linewidth enhancement factor
  • \(v_g\) = group velocity
  • In the moving frame of the pulse the equation becomes an ODE in z: the amplitude grows by g/2 and acquires a phase −αg/2.
  • The linewidth enhancement factor α (3–7 in SOAs) converts gain changes into phase changes; it is the same α_H that broadens laser linewidths.

Carrier Density Dynamics

Rate Equation:

\[\frac{\partial N}{\partial t} = \frac{I}{qV} - \frac{N}{\tau_c} - \frac{g(N)}{\hbar\omega_0}|A|^2\]

Physical Interpretation:

  • First term: carrier injection from current
  • Second term: recombination losses
  • Third term: stimulated emission (signal amplification)
  • Same rate equation as in the main section, now written with the optical intensity |A|²: the third term is the stimulated emission that depletes carriers.

Gain Dynamics

Modified with ASE:

\[\frac{\partial g}{\partial t} = \frac{g_0-g}{\tau_c} - \frac{g(|A|^2 + P_{\text{ASE}})}{E_{\text{sat}}}\]

Key Parameters:

  • \(g_0\) = unsaturated gain (current-dependent)
  • \(\tau_c\) = carrier lifetime
  • \(E_{\text{sat}}\) = saturation energy
  • \(P_{\text{ASE}}\) = ASE power
  • Writing the equation in terms of gain (using g linear in N) gives a single equation with a saturation energy E_sat.
  • The ASE term acts like a constant input power that keeps saturating the gain, which shortens the recovery time.

Part 2: Parameter Extraction

  • How to obtain the model parameters from simple measurements.

Overview of Key Parameters

Parameter Description Typical Range
\(\Gamma\) Confinement factor 0.3-0.7
\(a\) Gain cross-section 2.5-4.5×10⁻²⁰ m²
\(\tau_c\) Carrier lifetime 0.1-1 ns
\(E_{\text{sat}}\) Saturation energy 0.5-2 pJ
\(\alpha\) Linewidth enhancement 3-7
  • Check units: a = 2.5–4.5 × 10^-20 m² is the differential gain (dg/dN), equal to 2.5–4.5 × 10^-16 cm².

Step 1: CW Gain Measurements

Experimental Setup:

  1. Launch weak CW signal (no saturation)
  2. Measure output power vs. input power
  3. Vary drive current systematically
  4. Use narrow optical filter to reject ASE

Result: Small-signal gain \(G(I)\) vs. current \(I\)

  • The narrow filter is essential: without it the detector measures signal plus ASE and overestimates the gain.

Gain vs. Current Relationship

Low Current (No ASE):

\[\ln G = g_0(I) \cdot L = \Gamma a N_0 L\left(\frac{I}{I_0} - 1\right)\]

From Linear Region:

  • Slope → \(\Gamma a N_0 L\)
  • Intercept → transparency current \(I_0\)

High Current (ASE Saturation):

\[G(I) = \exp\left(\frac{g_0(I)L}{1 + P_{\text{ASE}}/P_{\text{sat}}}\right)\]

  • At low current ln G is linear in I above transparency: the slope and intercept give Γ a N_0 L and the transparency current I_0.
  • At high current the curve bends because of ASE self-saturation.

Step 2: Carrier Lifetime Model

Recombination Mechanisms:

\[\frac{1}{\tau_c} = A_{\text{nr}} + B_{\text{sp}}N + C_a N^2\]

Where:

  • \(A_{\text{nr}}\) = non-radiative recombination rate
  • \(B_{\text{sp}}\) = spontaneous recombination coefficient
  • \(C_a\) = Auger recombination coefficient

At steady state:

\[\frac{I}{qV} = N\left(A_{\text{nr}} + B_{\text{sp}}N + C_a N^2\right)\]

  • This is the ABC recombination model from the first slide of Section 0: non-radiative, radiative and Auger terms.
  • The carrier lifetime falls with carrier density, so the SOA gets faster at high current even without ASE.

Step 3: ASE Power Extraction

Key Insight: ASE scales with carrier density squared

\[P_{\text{ASE}} = D \cdot N^2\]

Extraction Method:

  1. Measure ASE spectrum at various currents
  2. Integrate over full bandwidth
  3. Account for coupling losses
  4. Fit to \(N^2\) dependence to extract \(D\)

Challenge: ASE spectrum is broad (>50 nm) and shifts with current

  • ASE scales as N² because spontaneous emission is bimolecular (BN² term).
  • Coupling losses must be measured separately; they are the main source of uncertainty.

Step 4: Physical Parameter Deduction

From Transparency Conditions:

\[N_0 = \frac{I_0 \tau_{c0}}{qV}\]

From Saturation Energy:

\[E_{\text{sat}} = \frac{\hbar\omega_0 \sigma}{\Gamma a}\]

Where \(\sigma\) = mode cross-section

Active Volume:

\[V = \sigma \cdot L\]

  • The saturation energy combines photon energy, mode area and differential gain: E_sat = P_sat × τ_c.

Example: Parameter Values

Assumed (from literature):

  • \(\Gamma = 0.4\)
  • \(a = 10.5 \times 10^{-20}\) m²
  • \(A_{\text{nr}} = 3.9 \times 10^9\) s⁻¹
  • \(B_{\text{sp}} = 8.5 \times 10^{-16}\) m³/s
  • \(C_a = 3.3 \times 10^{-40}\) m⁶/s

Deduced (from measurements):

  • \(N_0 = 1.64 \times 10^{23}\) m⁻³
  • \(V = 2.83 \times 10^{-16}\) m³
  • \(E_{\text{sat}} = 0.824\) pJ
  • \(D = 2.32 \times 10^{-50}\) m⁶W
  • Practise unit conversion with the class: N_0 = 1.64 × 10^23 m^-3 = 1.64 × 10^17 cm^-3; B_sp = 8.5 × 10^-16 m³/s = 8.5 × 10^-10 cm³/s, consistent with the B values of Section 0.

Part 3: ASE Effects

  • ASE as a beneficial effect for gain dynamics.

ASE-Induced Gain Recovery

Effective Recovery Time:

\[\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{1}{\tau_c} + \frac{1}{\tau_{\text{ASE}}}\]

Where:

\[\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{ASE}}} = \frac{P_{\text{ASE}}}{E_{\text{sat}}} = \frac{DN^2}{E_{\text{sat}}}\]

Key Result: ASE can reduce recovery time from 200 ps to 10 ps!

  • ASE adds a stimulated-emission recombination channel that works all the time, so the effective lifetime is shorter than the spontaneous carrier lifetime.
  • Trade-off: faster recovery, but more noise.

Recovery Time vs. Current

Physical Picture:

  • Low current: \(\tau_{\text{eff}} \approx \tau_c \approx 200\) ps
  • High current: ASE saturates gain continuously
  • At 500 mA: \(\tau_{\text{eff}} \approx 10\) ps

Implication: SOA behaves more like optical fiber at high currents

  • At high current the effective recovery time approaches 10 ps; the SOA then responds more like a fast medium and pattern effects decrease.

Impact on Nonlinear Phase Shift

SPM-Induced Phase:

\[\phi_{\text{nl}}(\tau) = -\frac{\alpha}{2}h(\tau)\]

Where \(h(\tau) = \int_0^L g(z,\tau)dz\)

Without ASE: Asymmetric phase profile

With ASE: Symmetric, pulse-like phase profile

  • Without ASE the gain recovers slowly after the pulse, so the phase profile is asymmetric. With fast recovery it follows the pulse shape more closely.

Frequency Chirp

Chirp Relation:

\[\nu_0(\tau) = \frac{\alpha}{4\pi}\frac{dh}{d\tau}\]

Effect of ASE:

  • Without ASE: Strong red chirp, weak blue chirp
  • With ASE: Nearly linear chirp across pulse
  • Benefit: Enables pulse compression
  • The chirp is the time derivative of the phase. Gain depletion raises the index and produces a red shift on the leading edge; fast recovery adds a blue shift on the trailing edge.
  • A nearly linear chirp can be compensated with dispersion, enabling pulse compression.

Part 4: Pulse Amplification

  • How pulse shape and spectrum change with the drive current.

Pulse Shape Evolution

Dimensionless Parameter:

\[r = \frac{\tau_p}{\tau_{\text{eff}}}\]

Where \(\tau_p\) = pulse width parameter

Low current (\(r \ll 1\)):

  • Asymmetric output pulse
  • Sharp leading edge, slow trailing edge

High current (\(r \sim 1\)):

  • More symmetric pulse shape
  • Both edges experience similar gain
  • The ratio r of pulse width to effective lifetime decides the regime. Small r: the leading edge depletes the gain and the trailing edge sees less gain (asymmetric). Large r: the gain partly recovers within the pulse.

Spectral Characteristics

Low Current (r < 0.1):

  • Highly asymmetric spectrum
  • Dominant red-shifted lobe
  • Minimal blue-shifted components

High Current (r > 0.3):

  • Progressive symmetrization
  • Growing blue-shifted lobe
  • More fiber-like behavior
  • Asymmetric spectra at low current reflect the dominant red chirp; at high current the blue-shifted lobe grows as the gain recovers faster.

Experimental Validation

Test Conditions:

  • Gain-switched DFB laser pulses
  • Wavelength: 1594.4 nm
  • Pulse width: 57 ps FWHM
  • Peak power: 3 mW
  • Current range: 40-500 mA

Agreement: Excellent match between theory and experiment

  • The agreement between model and measurement validates the extracted parameters; mention that the model is simple (no spectral hole burning or carrier heating), which limits it to pulses longer than a few ps.

Part 5: Practical Applications

  • Where fast SOAs are used.

Applications of Fast SOAs

Wavelength Conversion:

  • Cross-phase modulation
  • Four-wave mixing
  • Fast response critical

Signal Regeneration:

  • Reshaping distorted signals
  • Reducing noise
  • Pattern-effect mitigation

Pulse Reshaping:

  • Compression via chirp
  • Temporal gating
  • All-optical switching
  • Cross-gain and cross-phase modulation (XGM, XPM) copy data from one wavelength to another; this requires gain recovery faster than the bit period.

Design Considerations

For Fast Gain Recovery:

  1. High drive currents (up to 500 mA)
  2. Careful thermal management
  3. Optimized geometry (small V, high Γ)
  4. Material selection (high a)

Trade-offs:

  • Higher ASE noise
  • Increased power consumption
  • Thermal effects
  • High current, small volume and high differential gain all shorten the effective lifetime, but they increase ASE noise and heat.

Advantages vs. Fiber-Based Systems

SOA Advantages:

  • 1000× lower power requirements
  • Compact footprint (mm vs. km)
  • Potential for integration
  • Electronic control

Limitations:

  • ASE noise
  • Pattern effects (at low current)
  • Wavelength dependence
  • Fibre nonlinear devices need watts of power and km of fibre; SOAs work at mW and mm, but add ASE noise and patterning.

Measurement Techniques Summary

Essential Measurements:

  1. CW gain vs. current (small signal)
  2. ASE spectrum vs. current
  3. Pulse spectra at various currents
  4. Pulse shapes (optional, for validation)

Analysis Steps:

  1. Extract \(\Gamma aN_0L\) and \(I_0\) from linear regime
  2. Fit high-current data to extract \(D\)
  3. Calculate \(\tau_{\text{eff}}(I)\)
  4. Validate with pulse experiments
