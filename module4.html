A. PN Junctions: Physics

  • Section A: we need just enough semiconductor physics to predict how many carriers we move per volt, and how fast.

Introduction to PN Junctions

Why PN Junctions in Photonics?

  • Enable electrical control of optical properties
  • Modulate refractive index via carrier concentration
  • Fast response (GHz speeds possible)
  • Efficient and compact
  • CMOS fabrication compatible

Key application: Free carrier plasma dispersion effect in silicon

  • Silicon has no linear electro-optic (Pockels) effect (centrosymmetric crystal) and a weak Kerr effect → free carriers are the only practical fast mechanism.
  • The plasma dispersion effect: free carriers change both n and α (Drude-like, Kramers–Kronig).
  • ‘CMOS compatible’: same implants/anneals as transistors — this is why Si photonics won in datacom.

Semiconductor Fundamentals

Silicon Band Structure

Key parameters:

  • Band gap: \(E_g = 1.12\) eV (at 300 K)
  • Intrinsic carrier concentration: \(n_i = 1.45 \times 10^{10}\) cm⁻³
  • Electron mobility: \(\mu_n = 1400\) cm²/(V·s)
  • Hole mobility: \(\mu_p = 450\) cm²/(V·s)

Doping:

  • N-type: Phosphorus, Arsenic donors
  • P-type: Boron acceptors
  • Typical doping: \(10^{17}\) - \(10^{18}\) cm⁻³
  • Recall the numbers students will reuse in every calculation: E_g, n_i, μ_n, μ_p.
  • n_i = 1.45e10 cm⁻³ is the textbook value; the modern accepted value is ~1.0e10 (9.65e9). It shifts V_bi by ~20 mV — mention so they are not surprised by simulators (Lumerical CHARGE/Sentaurus use the newer value).
  • Mobilities are for low doping; at 1e17–1e18 they drop significantly (μ_n ≈ 800 → 250, μ_p ≈ 330 → 150 cm²/Vs). This matters for series resistance later.

PN Junction Formation

Built-in Potential

When p-type and n-type regions are joined:

\[V_{bi} = \frac{kT}{q} \ln\left(\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}\right)\]

Where:

  • \(k\) = Boltzmann constant (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K)
  • \(T\) = Temperature (K)
  • \(q\) = Elementary charge (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
  • \(N_A\) = Acceptor concentration (cm⁻³)
  • \(N_D\) = Donor concentration (cm⁻³)
  • \(n_i\) = Intrinsic carrier concentration

Typical value: \(V_{bi} \approx 0.7 - 0.9\) V for silicon

  • Physical picture: diffusion of majority carriers across the junction leaves ionised dopants → field → equilibrium when drift balances diffusion (Fermi levels align).
  • V_bi grows only logarithmically with doping: ×10 doping on both sides → +2·V_T·ln10 ≈ +0.12 V.
  • Use the figure to introduce sign conventions: forward bias reduces the barrier, reverse bias increases it. Be explicit about the sign convention used later (V > 0 = forward).

Carrier Dynamics

Drift and Diffusion

Drift current (due to electric field):

\[J_{drift} = q(\mu_n n + \mu_p p)E\]

Diffusion current (due to concentration gradient):

\[J_{diff,n} = qD_n \frac{dn}{dx}, \quad J_{diff,p} = -qD_p \frac{dp}{dx}\]

Einstein relation:

\[\frac{D}{\mu} = \frac{kT}{q} = V_T \approx 26 \text{ mV (at 300 K)}\]

Where \(D\) is diffusion coefficient and \(\mu\) is mobility.

  • Two transport mechanisms: drift (field) and diffusion (gradient); in equilibrium they cancel separately for e and h.
  • Einstein relation links them: D = μ·V_T. E.g. D_n ≈ 36 cm²/s, D_p ≈ 12 cm²/s at low doping.
  • Point out the sign difference in the diffusion terms (electrons have negative charge).

Carrier Recombination

Recombination Mechanisms

  1. Radiative recombination (indirect bandgap in Si - negligible) \[\tau_r \sim 1 \text{ ms}\]

  2. Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) (via traps) \[\tau_{SRH} \sim 1 - 10 \text{ μs}\]

  3. Auger recombination (three-particle process) \[\tau_{Auger} \sim 0.1 - 1 \text{ μs at high injection}\]

Effective minority carrier lifetime:

\[\frac{1}{\tau_{eff}} = \frac{1}{\tau_r} + \frac{1}{\tau_{SRH}} + \frac{1}{\tau_{Auger}}\]

  • Lifetime sets the speed of injection devices (part B): carriers can only be removed by recombination or extraction.
  • The bulk values here (μs) are not what we get in waveguides: in 220 nm SOI ribs, surface recombination at the etched sidewalls and at the BOX interface dominates → τ_eff ~ 1–10 ns. This is the value used in the injection example later — say it explicitly, otherwise the two slides look contradictory.
  • Auger matters only at high injection (>1e18), i.e. precisely the injection-modulator regime.

Carrier Dynamics: Time Response

Transit Time and RC Limitations

Carrier transit time:

\[\tau_{transit} = \frac{W^2}{2D}\]

Where \(W\) is the depletion width and \(D\) is the ambipolar diffusion coefficient.

For fast modulation:

  • Minimize depletion width \(W\)
  • Maximize carrier mobility
  • Typical transit time: 10-100 ps

Speed is often RC-limited rather than transit-limited!

  • Diffusion transit W²/2D applies to neutral regions; inside the depletion region carriers drift at ~v_sat ≈ 1e7 cm/s → W/v_sat ≈ 3 ps for 300 nm. So depletion devices are never transit-limited.
  • Key message to keep: in depletion modulators the limit is RC (and photon lifetime for rings); in injection modulators it is the carrier lifetime.

Depletion Region

Depletion Width

At equilibrium (V = 0):

\[W = \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_s V_{bi}}{q}\left(\frac{N_A + N_D}{N_A N_D}\right)}\]

Under applied bias V:

\[W(V) = \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_s (V_{bi} - V)}{q}\left(\frac{N_A + N_D}{N_A N_D}\right)}\]

Where:

  • \(\epsilon_s\) = Silicon permittivity = 11.7 \(\epsilon_0\) = 1.04 × 10⁻¹² F/cm
  • \(V\) = Applied voltage: \(V > 0\) forward, \(V < 0\) reverse bias (convention used throughout)
  • Derive W from Poisson with the depletion approximation (charge neutrality: N_A·x_p = N_D·x_n). Worth doing on the chalkboard.
  • Note the depletion extends mostly into the lightly-doped side — relevant for asymmetric junctions in the assignment.
  • Sign convention: with W ∝ √(V_bi − V), reverse bias is V < 0 (the example uses V = −2 V). Same convention in the code, the Python figures and the interactive slide.

Depletion Region Characteristics

Electric Field Distribution

Maximum electric field (at junction):

\[E_{max} = \sqrt{\frac{2q(V_{bi} - V)}{\epsilon_s}\frac{N_A N_D}{N_A + N_D}}\]

For symmetric junction (\(N_A = N_D = N\)):

\[W = \sqrt{\frac{4\epsilon_s (V_{bi} - V)}{qN}}\]

\[E_{max} = \frac{2(V_{bi} - V)}{W}\]

  • Triangular field profile; E_max at the metallurgical junction.
  • Sanity check against breakdown: Si breakdown field ~3–5×10⁵ V/cm at these dopings, so drive voltages of a few V are safe for ~1e17–1e18 junctions, but highly doped (1e18+) junctions limit the reverse bias.

Extra: the depletion approximation from Poisson’s equation

Assumption: inside \(-x_p < x < x_n\) all carriers are swept out, outside the material is neutral.

\[\frac{dE}{dx} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_s}, \qquad \rho = \begin{cases} -qN_A & -x_p<x<0 \\ +qN_D & 0<x<x_n \end{cases}\]

1. Charge neutrality: \(N_A x_p = N_D x_n\) → the depletion extends into the lighter side.

2. Field: linear on each side, maximum at the junction

\[E_{max} = \frac{qN_A x_p}{\epsilon_s} = \frac{qN_D x_n}{\epsilon_s}\]

3. Voltage = area of the triangle: \(V_{bi} - V = \tfrac{1}{2}E_{max}W\), with \(W = x_n + x_p\).

Eliminating \(x_p, x_n\):

\[W = \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_s (V_{bi} - V)}{q}\,\frac{N_A + N_D}{N_A N_D}}\]

Charge per area \(Q_d = qN_A x_p\) → differential capacitance

\[C_j = \frac{dQ_d}{dV} = \frac{\epsilon_s}{W}\]

Beyond the abrupt model:

  • Implanted + annealed junctions are graded: for a linear grade \(W \propto (V_{bi}-V)^{1/3}\)
  • Free-carrier tails (Debye length \(L_D = \sqrt{\epsilon_s V_T/qN}\) ≈ 7 nm at 3×10¹⁷) smooth the edges: TCAD (Lumerical CHARGE, Sentaurus) solves the full drift-diffusion problem
  • Do this on the chalkboard: three lines (neutrality, triangle, area) give everything.
  • The point of the capacitance line: the charge moved by the voltage is exactly the charge that changes the optical index. Capacitance and modulation efficiency are the same physics — the root of the speed/efficiency trade-off.
  • Debye length tells students when the abrupt-edge picture fails: when W is only a few L_D (very high doping, near forward bias).

Junction Capacitance

Depletion Capacitance

Per unit area:

\[C_j = \frac{\epsilon_s}{W} = \sqrt{\frac{q\epsilon_s}{2(V_{bi}-V)}\frac{N_A N_D}{N_A + N_D}}\]

Voltage dependence:

\[C_j(V) = \frac{C_{j0}}{\sqrt{1 - V/V_{bi}}}\]

Where \(C_{j0}\) is the capacitance at V = 0.

  • Junction behaves as a parallel-plate capacitor of thickness W: C_j = ε_s/W.
  • C ∝ (V_bi − V)^(-1/2) → capacitance falls with reverse bias: reverse-biasing helps speed. This is also why a depletion modulator is a varactor (nonlinear C → nonlinear phase vs V).
  • Same sign convention as before: V < 0 reverse.

Junction Capacitance (continued)

Total Capacitance

For a PN junction modulator:

\[C_{total} = C_j + C_{diff} + C_{parasitic}\]

Diffusion capacitance (forward bias only):

\[C_{diff} = \frac{q}{kT}\,\tau\, I_F = \frac{\tau\, I_F}{V_T}\]

(\(I_F\) forward current, \(\tau\) minority-carrier lifetime: stored charge \(Q_s = \tau I_F\), \(C_{diff} = dQ_s/dV\))

Parasitic capacitance:

  • Interconnect capacitance
  • Pad capacitance
  • Typically 10-50 fF
  • C_diff only matters in forward bias (injection devices) — it is what makes injection devices slow AND hard to drive.
  • C_diff = τ·I_F/V_T follows from the stored charge Q_s = τ·I_F and I_F ∝ exp(V/V_T). Number: τ = 1 ns, I_F = 5 mA → C_diff = 1e-9 × 5e-3 / 0.026 ≈ 190 pF — four orders of magnitude above a depletion junction (~10–100 fF). That is why injection devices are slow and hard to drive.
  • Parasitics (pads, routing) of 10–50 fF are comparable to or larger than a ring junction (~5–20 fF) → co-integration/flip-chip matters.

Capacitance: Design Impact

Speed-Efficiency Trade-off

RC time constant:

\[\tau_{RC} = R \times C_{total}\]

3-dB bandwidth:

\[f_{3dB} = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}\]

Key design principle:

  • Large junction area → High efficiency, but high capacitance
  • Small junction area → Low capacitance, but low efficiency
  • Optimization required!
  • f_3dB = 1/(2πRC) for a single pole; R includes driver output, contacts, and the doped-Si series resistance (often dominant).
  • The trade-off stated as “area” is really length for a waveguide modulator: more length → more phase but more C (for lumped devices).
  • Preview: for long MZMs the lumped RC model breaks down → traveling-wave (part E).

Plasma Dispersion Effect

Optical Index Change in Silicon

Soref-Bennett equations (λ = 1.55 μm):

\[\Delta n = \Delta n_e + \Delta n_h = -[8.8 \times 10^{-22} \Delta N_e + 8.5 \times 10^{-18} (\Delta N_h)^{0.8}]\]

\[\Delta \alpha = \Delta \alpha_e + \Delta \alpha_h = 8.5 \times 10^{-18} \Delta N_e + 6.0 \times 10^{-18} \Delta N_h \text{ [cm}^{-1}\text{]}\]

Where:

  • \(\Delta N_e\) = Free electron concentration change (cm⁻³)
  • \(\Delta N_h\) = Free hole concentration change (cm⁻³)
  • \(\Delta n\) = Refractive index change
  • \(\Delta \alpha\) = Absorption coefficient change (cm⁻¹)
  • Soref & Bennett 1987 (empirical fit to Drude + absorption data; later refined by Nedeljkovic et al. 2011 for 1.3–14 μm).
  • Coefficients are for 1550 nm; at 1310 nm they are different (smaller Δn and Δα, roughly ∝ λ²). Matters because several later examples are at 1310.
  • Note the 0.8 exponent for holes: sub-linear.

Plasma Dispersion: Key Insights

Design Implications

  1. Holes give more Δn per carrier: at 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, \(|\Delta n_h| \approx 2.1\times10^{-3}\) vs \(|\Delta n_e| \approx 8.8\times10^{-4}\)
  2. Holes also absorb less: \(6.0\) vs \(8.5 \times 10^{-18}\) cm² → better \(\Delta n/\Delta\alpha\)
  3. Both effects increase with carrier density
  4. Trade-off: Strong modulation vs. high loss
  5. Figure of merit: \(\frac{\Delta n}{\Delta \alpha}\) should be maximized

Typical operating range:

  • Carrier density: \(10^{17}\) - \(10^{18}\) cm⁻³
  • Index change: \(\Delta n \sim 10^{-4}\) - \(10^{-3}\)
  • Additional loss: \(\sim\) 5-50 dB/cm
  • Holes win twice: ~2.4× more index change per carrier at 1e18 cm⁻³ AND less absorption (6.0e-18 vs 8.5e-18 cm²). (The ratio 8.5e-18/8.8e-22 ≈ 10⁴ often quoted is a ratio of coefficients with different units, not of contributions.)
  • Design consequence (this is the real take-away): holes are ‘better’ carriers → many depletion designs offset the junction so the P-side depletion sits in the mode center, or use higher P doping.
  • FOM Δn/Δα ↔︎ the V_π·α_L (V·dB) product used to compare modulators — good to introduce the term here.

Change of index in silicon

  • Use the plots to read off orders of magnitude: 1e17 → Δn ~1e-4; 1e18 → ~1e-3.
  • Point at the hole curve being above the electron curve for Δn (consistent with the previous slide).

Python: Soref–Bennett at 1310 and 1550 nm

SOREF = {  # a_e, a_h, exponent, b_e, b_h
    1.55: (8.8e-22, 8.5e-18, 0.8, 8.5e-18, 6.0e-18),
    1.31: (6.2e-22, 6.0e-18, 0.8, 6.0e-18, 4.0e-18),
}
def soref(dNe, dNh, lam=1.55):
    ae, ah, p, be, bh = SOREF[lam]
    dn = -(ae * dNe + ah * dNh**p)
    dalpha = be * dNe + bh * dNh          # cm^-1
    return dn, dalpha
  • Holes give more \(|\Delta n|\) and less loss at all practical densities → the best FOM.
  • At 1310 nm both effects are ~30 % weaker (Drude: \(\propto \lambda^2\)) → O-band modulators need more voltage or length.
  • Loss in dB/cm = 4.34 × Δα (cm⁻¹): 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ of each carrier costs ≈ 60 dB/cm.
  • The FOM panel is the quantitative version of the “holes are better” statement.
  • The 1310 coefficients are Soref & Bennett’s fit at 1.3 µm. Nedeljkovic et al. (2011) extended the fits to the mid-IR; use those beyond 1.6 µm.
  • Ask: why is the hole term sub-linear (exponent 0.8)? It is empirical — the Drude model (next slide) predicts linear behaviour.

Extra: where plasma dispersion comes from (Drude model)

Free carriers respond to the optical field like damped oscillators without restoring force:

\[m^* \ddot{x} + \frac{m^*}{\tau_c}\dot{x} = -qE\]

This adds a negative susceptibility → index decreases and absorption increases with carrier density:

\[\Delta n = -\frac{q^2 \lambda^2}{8\pi^2 c^2 \epsilon_0 n}\left(\frac{\Delta N_e}{m^*_{ce}} + \frac{\Delta N_h}{m^*_{ch}}\right)\]

\[\Delta \alpha = \frac{q^3 \lambda^2}{4\pi^2 c^3 \epsilon_0 n}\left(\frac{\Delta N_e}{m^{*2}_{ce}\mu_e} + \frac{\Delta N_h}{m^{*2}_{ch}\mu_h}\right)\]

Check the order of magnitude (electrons, \(m^*_{ce} = 0.26\,m_0\), \(n = 3.48\), λ = 1.55 µm):

\[\frac{\Delta n}{\Delta N_e} \approx -1.2\times10^{-21}\ \text{cm}^3\]

vs. the empirical \(-8.8\times10^{-22}\): Drude gets the size right.

Why Soref & Bennett is used instead:

  • Measured absorption spectra + Kramers–Kronig, includes inter-valence-band effects for holes
  • Hence the sub-linear hole term \((\Delta N_h)^{0.8}\)
  • Both effects scale with \(\lambda^2\): stronger in the mid-IR
  • The mobility in Δα explains why lightly doped (high-mobility) silicon absorbs less per carrier: damping τ_c = m*μ/q.
  • Kramers–Kronig: Δn and Δα are two faces of the same susceptibility; you cannot have one without the other → every carrier-based modulator has amplitude–phase coupling (chirp).
  • Reference: R. Soref & B. Bennett, IEEE JQE 23, 123 (1987).

Introduction to Phase Shifters

Role in Photonic Circuits

Applications:

  • Modulators (MZM, MRR)
  • Switches and routers
  • Phase shifters for beamforming
  • Tunable filters
  • Optical phased arrays

Key metrics:

  • Phase shift efficiency (\(V_\pi L\))
  • Speed (bandwidth)
  • Insertion loss
  • Power consumption
  • Phase is the universal currency: amplitude modulators, switches, filters, OPAs all reduce to phase shifters.
  • Define each metric precisely: V_πL (V·cm), P_π (mW), bandwidth (GHz), loss (dB/cm or dB), and the combined FOM V_π·L·α.

Phase Shift Mechanisms

Three Main Approaches

Mechanism Speed Efficiency Power
Carrier Injection ~0.1–1 GHz (more with pre-emphasis) Excellent (\(V_\pi L\) ≈ 0.01–0.05 V·cm) mW (DC current)
Carrier Depletion >40 GHz Moderate (\(V_\pi L\) ≈ 1.5–3 V·cm) Low (no DC current)
Thermo-Optic ~10–100 kHz Large Δn (\(P_\pi\) ≈ 1–30 mW) High

Phase shift:

\[\Delta \phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \Delta n_{eff} L\]

Where \(L\) is the phase shifter length and \(\Delta n_{eff}\) is the effective index change.

  • Δφ = k₀·Δn_eff·L: phase = index × length, so efficiency can always be traded for length.
  • Thermo-optic ‘efficiency excellent’ means large Δn (dn/dT·ΔT ~1e-3 easily) at the cost of power and speed.
  • Speed column: thermal time constants of 1–10 μs (standard heaters) correspond to f ≈ 1/(2πτ) ≈ 16–160 kHz; undercut heaters are ~10× slower.

Carrier Injection Phase Shifter

Forward-Biased PN Junction

Principle:

  • Forward bias injects carriers
  • Increases carrier density in waveguide
  • Changes refractive index via plasma effect
  • Moderate speed (limited by carrier lifetime)

Advantages:

  • High efficiency
  • Low voltage operation
  • Simple fabrication

Disadvantages:

  • Limited speed (~1 GHz)
  • High optical loss
  • Power consumption in ON state
  • PIN diode (intrinsic waveguide core, P and N in the slab) forward biased: carriers flood the core → large Δn at ~1 V.
  • Speed limited by τ_eff (~ns) → ~100s MHz natively; GHz possible with pre-emphasis (Xu/Lipson 2007 12.5 Gb/s ring).
  • Absorption loss rises with injection → amplitude chirp.

Typical structure

  • Describe the rib: 220 nm core, ~90 nm slab, P++/N++ contacts placed ≥ ~0.5–1 μm from the core to avoid absorption.
  • Ask students: why is the intrinsic region the core? (maximize overlap where carriers accumulate).

Carrier Injection: Analysis

Carrier Distribution

Injected carrier density:

\[\Delta N = \frac{J \tau}{q W}\]

Where:

  • \(J\) = Current density (A/cm²)
  • \(\tau\) = Carrier lifetime (s)
  • \(W\) = Injection region width (cm)

Index change:

\[\Delta n_{eff} = \Gamma \Delta n\]

Where \(\Gamma\) is the optical mode overlap with the carrier injection region.

  • Continuity in steady state: injected = recombined → ΔN = Jτ/(qW).
  • Careful with geometry: in a lateral PIN, current flows sideways; the relevant area is (rib height × L), and W is the intrinsic width in the current direction.
  • Γ is the fraction of modal energy in the carrier region (not a field overlap).

Extra: injection dynamics and the PIN diode

Rate equation (intrinsic region of volume \(V_i = w h L\)):

\[\frac{d\Delta N}{dt} = \frac{I(t)}{qV_i} - \frac{\Delta N}{\tau}\]

  • Step response: \(\Delta N(t) = \Delta N_\infty(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\)
  • 10–90 % rise time \(= 2.2\,\tau\); \(f_{3dB} = 1/(2\pi\tau)\)
  • τ in SOI ribs: 0.5–5 ns, set by surface recombination at the etched sidewalls and the BOX interface

I–V: \(I = I_s(e^{V/nV_T} - 1)\) → \(\Delta\phi\) grows logarithmically with V but linearly with I → drive with current.

Ways to go faster:

  1. Pre-emphasis / reverse kick – extract carriers with a reverse pulse
  2. Reduce τ – ion implantation damage, closer contacts (but more leakage, less efficiency: \(I_\pi \propto 1/\tau\))
  3. Accept it – injection for VOAs, switches, slow tuning

Efficiency–speed product: \(I_\pi \cdot \tau \propto qV_i\,\Delta N_\pi\) is constant for a given geometry: halving τ doubles the current.

  • Key physical point: in forward bias the junction stores charge (diffusion capacitance C_diff = τI/V_T ≈ 190 pF at 5 mA): an RC view gives the same time constant τ.
  • The I·τ product is the injection analogue of the depletion V_πL–C trade-off.
  • Diffusion length in SOI: L_d = √(Dτ) ≈ 1–2 µm for τ ~ ns: carriers stay near the intrinsic region, which is why the contacts can sit ~1 µm away.

Carrier Depletion Phase Shifter

Reverse-Biased PN Junction

Principle:

  • Reverse bias depletes carriers
  • Modulates depletion width
  • Changes effective index
  • Very fast (capacitance-limited)

Advantages:

  • High speed (>40 GHz)
  • Low optical loss
  • No DC power in static state

Disadvantages:

  • Lower efficiency than injection
  • Higher drive voltage
  • More complex design
  • Reverse-biased PN: no current flows (only leakage) → speed set by RC, not lifetime.
  • Only carriers inside the moving edge of the depletion region contribute → small Δn per volt → longer devices / higher V.
  • ‘No DC power’: true; but drivers dissipate power (later).

Carrier Depletion: Analysis

Depletion-Based Index Change

Mechanism:

  1. Depletion region expands/contracts with voltage
  2. Carrier density changes in depletion region
  3. Index change via plasma dispersion

Effective index change:

\[\Delta n_{eff} = \Gamma_{depl} \int \frac{\partial n}{\partial N} \Delta N(x) dx\]

Typical performance (lateral junction):

  • \(V_\pi L\) ≈ 1.5–3 V·cm (≈ 1 V·cm for L/U-shaped or interleaved junctions)
  • Speed: >40 GHz (with proper design)
  • Doping loss: ≈ 2–10 dB/cm (depends on doping, see Python slide)
  • ΔQ = C·ΔV: the carriers removed per volt ARE the junction capacitance charge. So efficiency and capacitance are tied — this is the fundamental depletion trade-off.
  • The slide formula mixes Γ and the integral. Cleaner form (first-order perturbation): Δn_eff ≈ ∬ Δn(x,y)|E|² dA / ∬|E|² dA (strictly weighted by n/n_g factors for high-contrast guides); in practice the mode solver recomputes n_eff with the perturbed index map.
  • Typical: V_πL 1.5–3 V·cm, doping loss a few dB/cm at 3e17 (our model: 1.97 V·cm and 4.9 dB/cm).

Depletion Phase Shifter Design

Lateral PN Junction Configuration

Common geometries:

  1. Lateral PN

    • Doped regions side-by-side (Easy fabrication)
    • Moderate efficiency \(\rightarrow \Gamma\) ~ 0.3-0.5

  2. Interleaved PN

    • Multiple junctions (More complex fabrication)
    • Higher efficiency \(\rightarrow \Gamma\) ~ 0.6-0.8

  3. Vertical PN

    • Stacked doping(Requires selective epitaxy)
    • Highest efficiency possible \(\rightarrow \Gamma\) ~ 0.7-0.9
  • Lateral: easiest (two implants), what most PDKs offer.
  • Interleaved: junctions along propagation → more junction area per length (better efficiency, more C).
  • Vertical / L-shaped / U-shaped: junction parallel to the wafer → more mode overlap; vertical can be done with tilted/multi-energy implants, not necessarily epitaxy.
  • The Γ numbers are indicative; emphasize the trend rather than the values.

Depletion Phase Shifter: Optimization

Design Trade-offs: Doping concentration:

  • High doping (\(\gtrsim10^{18}\) cm⁻³):
    • Thinner depletion region → higher capacitance
    • More carriers swept per volt → better efficiency (lower \(V_\pi L\))
    • Higher optical loss
    • Lower series resistance
  • Low doping (\(\sim10^{17}\) cm⁻³):
    • Wider depletion region → lower capacitance
    • Fewer carriers per volt → worse \(V_\pi L\)
    • Lower optical loss
    • Higher series resistance (R·C roughly constant)

Optimal design:

  • Doping: \(3-5 \times 10^{17}\) cm⁻³
  • Depletion width: 200-400 nm
  • Balance speed and efficiency
  • Trade-off triangle: doping ↑ ⇒ V_πL ↓, loss ↑, C ↑, R ↓. Because C ∝ √N and R ∝ 1/(μN), the intrinsic R·C even improves slightly with doping; the real limit is optical loss.
  • Common misconception to address: “higher doping = lower C” is wrong (C = ε/W and W shrinks).
  • The ‘optimal’ 3–5e17 with W ~200–400 nm is a reasonable sweet spot; let students find it in the assignment.

def depletion_ps(NA, ND, V, x0=0, lam=1.55, w0=0.28e-4, gy=0.8):
    ae, ah, p, be, bh = SOREF[lam]
    ov = lambda x1, x2: 0.5*(erf(np.sqrt(2)*x2/w0)
                            - erf(np.sqrt(2)*x1/w0))  # Gaussian overlap
    _, xp0, xn0 = depletion(NA, ND, 0.0)
    _, xp, xn = depletion(NA, ND, V)

    alpha = gy*(be*ND*ov(x0 + xn, np.inf) + bh*NA*ov(-np.inf, x0 - xp))

Extra: the V_πL·α figure of merit and the doping optimum

The loss you pay for a given phase at drive \(V\):

\[L = \frac{V_\pi L}{V}\cdot\frac{\Delta\phi}{\pi} \;\Rightarrow\; IL_{dB} = \alpha_{dB}\,L = \frac{(V_\pi L \cdot \alpha_{dB})}{V}\,\frac{\Delta\phi}{\pi}\]

→ the product \(V_\pi L\cdot\alpha\) (V·dB) is the loss for a π shift at 1 V.

N (cm⁻³) \(V_\pi L\cdot\alpha\) (V·dB) L for π at 2 V
1×10¹⁷ 3.8 17 mm
3×10¹⁷ 9.7 10 mm
1×10¹⁸ 25 6 mm

Paradox: the FOM prefers low doping, yet everyone uses ~3–5×10¹⁷. Why?

  1. Length is not free: a TW electrode loses RF power ∝ √f · L → bandwidth falls with L (section E)
  2. Footprint and capacitance (total C ∝ L) → driver power
  3. Nobody drives a full π: Si MZMs run at 20–50 % of \(V_\pi\)

So the optimum is set by bandwidth × loss × footprint, not by one FOM.

Tricks that beat the lateral trade-off: offset / L-shaped / U-shaped / interleaved junctions, compensation doping near the core, SISCAP (MOS capacitor, ~0.2 V·cm).

  • This is the type of reasoning expected in the assignment’s “find optimal design point” section: state the constraint that fixes L (usually bandwidth), then minimise loss.
  • SISCAP (Intel/Lightwire): a thin oxide between poly-Si and Si, accumulation mode, much higher charge per volt — but higher C.

Thermo-Optic Phase Shifters

Temperature-Based Index Change

Thermo-optic coefficient of silicon:

\[\frac{dn}{dT} = 1.86 \times 10^{-4} \text{ K}^{-1} \text{ (at 1550 nm)}\]

Phase shift:

\[\Delta \phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \frac{dn}{dT} \Delta T L\]

Power for π phase shift:

\[P_\pi = \frac{\Delta T_\pi}{R_{th}}, \qquad \Delta T_\pi = \frac{\lambda}{2L\,dn/dT}\]

\(R_{th}\) thermal resistance (K/W). Since \(\Delta T_\pi L = \lambda/(2\,dn/dT) \approx 4.2\) K·mm and \(R_{th} \propto 1/L\), \(P_\pi\) does not depend on the heater length.

  • dn/dT of Si is large (1.86e-4 /K) — ~2× SiN and ~20× SiO₂.
  • Key insight: ΔT_π·L = λ/(2 dn/dT) ≈ 4.2 K·mm is fixed, and R_th ∝ 1/L, so P_π is independent of length — it only depends on the thermal resistance per unit length (heater design, trenches, undercut). Longer heaters run cooler, not cheaper.

Extra: why P_π does not depend on the heater length

Steady-state heat flow (2D cross-section, uniform along z):

\[P = G'\,L\,\Delta T, \qquad G' = \frac{1}{R_{th}L} \ \ [\text{W/(m·K)}]\]

Phase: \(\Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\frac{dn}{dT}\Delta T\,L\)

\[\Rightarrow\ \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\frac{dn}{dT}\,\frac{P}{G'}\]

Length cancels: a longer heater spreads the same power over more waveguide at lower temperature.

Estimate of G′ (heater width \(w\), oxide thickness \(t_{ox}\) to the substrate, heat spreading at ~45°):

\[G' \approx k_{SiO_2}\,\frac{w + 2t_{ox}}{t_{ox}}\quad (k_{SiO_2} = 1.4\ \text{W/mK})\]

Example: \(w = 2\) µm, \(t_{ox} = 3\) µm → \(G' ≈ 3.7\) W/(m·K), \(R_{th}L ≈ 0.27\) K·m/W → \(P_\pi ≈ 15\) mW.

How to lower P_π:

  • Increase \(t_{ox}\) / remove the substrate path (undercut) → \(G'\) ↓
  • Trenches next to the heater cut lateral spreading
  • Multi-pass (fold the waveguide under one heater): the light crosses the hot region several times → \(P_\pi / N_{pass}\)

Price: thermal capacitance per length is set by the heated volume, so \(\tau = C'_{th}/G'\) grows as \(G'\) falls: P_π × τ ≈ constant.

  • Derivation is three lines; the conclusion surprises most students.
  • Multi-pass/folded heaters are the one trick that really beats the length independence (the light sees the same hot region N times).
  • Thermal crosstalk is the same physics: the heat spreading that makes G′ large also heats the neighbours; trenches help both.

Introduction to Microring Modulators

Why Microrings?

  • Compact: ~5-10 μm radius
  • Low power: fJ/bit possible
  • CMOS compatible: Standard fabrication
  • Wavelength selective: Built-in WDM capability
  • High speed: >50 GHz demonstrated

Applications:

  • Dense WDM transceivers
  • Optical interconnects
  • Energy-efficient data centers
  • Footprint ~100× smaller than an MZM; energy fJ/bit because C is tiny.
  • The price: thermal sensitivity (~70 pm/K) and narrow optical bandwidth → need active stabilization.
  • WDM ‘for free’: each ring addresses one wavelength on a shared bus.

Microring Resonator Basics

Fundamental Parameters

Resonance condition:

\[m\lambda = 2\pi r n_{eff}\]

Where:

  • \(m\) = Integer mode number
  • \(\lambda\) = Resonance wavelength
  • \(r\) = Ring radius
  • \(n_{eff}\) = Effective refractive index

Free spectral range (FSR):

\[FSR = \frac{\lambda^2}{2\pi r n_g}\]

Where \(n_g = n_{eff} - \lambda \frac{dn_{eff}}{d\lambda}\) is the group index.

  • Resonance: integer number of wavelengths around the ring (uses n_eff); FSR uses n_g (dispersion!).
  • Quick number: r = 5 μm, n_g = 4.2 → FSR ≈ 18 nm at 1550.
  • Students often confuse n_eff and n_g — insist on it.

Microring Coupling Theory

Transfer Function

Power coupling coefficient \(\kappa\) per coupler:

All-pass ring (one bus), through port:

\[T = \left| \frac{t - a e^{j\phi}}{1 - at e^{j\phi}} \right|^2\]

Add-drop ring (two identical couplers), drop port:

\[D = \left| \frac{\kappa \sqrt{a} e^{j\phi/2}}{1 - a t^2 e^{j\phi}} \right|^2\]

Where:

  • \(t = \sqrt{1-\kappa}\) = self-coupling coefficient
  • \(a = e^{-\alpha L/2}\) = round-trip amplitude loss
  • \(\phi = 2\pi n_{eff} L / \lambda\) = round-trip phase
  • \(L = 2\pi r\) = ring circumference
  • A single-bus (all-pass) ring has no drop port. The add-drop ring has two couplers, so the light circulating sees t₁t₂ per round trip: general form D = κ₁κ₂·a / |1 − t₁t₂a·e^{jφ}|²; the slide shows the symmetric case t₁ = t₂ = t.
  • Derivation on the “Extra” slide below (field continuity at the coupler + round-trip feedback).
  • Define a carefully: amplitude round-trip transmission, α the power attenuation coefficient.

Quality Factor and Finesse

Resonance Characteristics

Quality factor:

\[Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\Delta \omega} = \frac{\lambda_0}{\Delta \lambda}\]

Loaded Q:

\[Q_L \approx \frac{2\pi n_g L}{\lambda_0} \cdot \frac{1}{\alpha L + \kappa}, \qquad L = 2\pi r\]

Finesse:

\[\mathcal{F} = \frac{FSR}{FWHM}\]

Typical values:

  • Q: 10,000 – 100,000 → FWHM ≈ 155 – 15 pm at 1550 nm
  • With FSR ≈ 10 nm: Finesse ≈ 65 – 650
  • Q = λ/Δλ; physically Q = 2π × (stored energy)/(energy lost per optical cycle).
  • Q_L ≈ 2π n_g L / [λ (αL + κ)]: the round-trip loss αL and the coupling κ add up — intrinsic and external losses, 1/Q_L = 1/Q_i + 1/Q_e.
  • Finesse F = FSR/FWHM = Q·FSR/λ = number of round trips (×2π) before the light decays.

Critical Coupling

Maximum Extinction

Critical coupling condition:

\[\kappa_{critical} = \alpha L\]

At critical coupling:

  • Through port extinction is maximum
  • \(T_{min} = 0\) (theoretically)
  • Drop port power is maximum

Under-coupled: \(\kappa < \alpha L\) → Poor extinction
Over-coupled: \(\kappa > \alpha L\) → Broad resonance
Critical: \(\kappa = \alpha L\) → Optimal for switching

  • Critical coupling: coupling loss = intrinsic loss → destructive interference is complete at the through port.
  • Both under- and over-coupling reduce extinction; under-coupled is narrower, over-coupled broader (lower loaded Q, faster).
  • Modulators are usually designed slightly over-coupled: robust to fab variations and faster.
  • Mention the ambiguity: from a through-port spectrum alone you cannot tell under- from over-coupled (need phase or a sweep).

Extra: derivation of the ring transfer function

Coupler (lossless, fields \(a\) = in, \(b\) = out):

\[\begin{pmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} t & j\kappa_f \\ j\kappa_f & t\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2\end{pmatrix},\quad t^2 + \kappa_f^2 = 1\]

Round trip: \(a_2 = a\,e^{-j\phi}\,b_2\)

Solve: \(b_2 = \dfrac{j\kappa_f\, a_1}{1 - t a e^{-j\phi}}\), then

\[\frac{b_1}{a_1} = \frac{t - a e^{-j\phi}}{1 - a t e^{-j\phi}}\]

(the slide uses \(e^{+j\phi}\): same |T|, opposite phase convention)

Near resonance (\(\phi = 2\pi m + \delta\), small losses) → Lorentzian:

\[T(\delta) \approx 1 - \frac{(1-a^2)(1-t^2)}{(1-at)^2 + at\,\delta^2}\]

  • FWHM in phase: \(\delta_{1/2} = 2(1-at)/\sqrt{at}\)
  • \(\mathcal{F} = 2\pi/\delta_{1/2}\), \(Q_L = \mathcal{F}\cdot \lambda/FSR\)

Q budget: \(\dfrac{1}{Q_L} = \dfrac{1}{Q_i} + \dfrac{1}{Q_e}\), with \(Q_i = \dfrac{2\pi n_g}{\lambda\,\alpha}\), \(Q_e = \dfrac{2\pi n_g L}{\lambda\,\kappa}\)

Critical coupling: \(Q_e = Q_i\), \(T_{min} = 0\).

  • The derivation takes five minutes on the board and explains everything that follows (critical coupling, Q, phase).
  • Q_i = 2π n_g/(λα) is independent of the radius — the intrinsic Q only measures loss per unit length. Example: 3 dB/cm (α = 0.69 cm⁻¹) → Q_i ≈ 2.5×10⁵ at 1550 nm.
  • Same algebra for the add-drop ring with a second coupler in the loop (t → t₁t₂ in the denominator).

Operating Principles: Modulation

Resonance Shift Modulation

Basic concept:

  1. Ring resonance aligned with signal wavelength (ON state)
  2. Apply voltage to phase shifter in ring
  3. Shift resonance away from signal (OFF state)
  4. Amplitude modulation achieved

Round-trip phase change required: about one linewidth, not π:

\[\Delta \phi \approx \frac{2\pi}{\mathcal{F}} \quad (\approx 0.05\text{–}0.1 \text{ rad})\]

Resonance shift (phase shifter of length \(L_{PS}\) in a ring of length \(L\)):

\[\Delta \lambda_{res} = \frac{\lambda_0\, \Delta n_{eff}}{n_g}\,\frac{L_{PS}}{L}\]

The resonance enhances the phase by ~\(\mathcal{F}/\pi\): that is why a 30 µm ring does what a mm-long MZM does.

  • Mechanism: shift the resonance by ~one linewidth so the laser goes from ‘notch’ to ‘pass’.
  • A ring does not need Δφ = π: shifting by one FWHM corresponds to a round-trip phase of 2π/F. With F ≈ 100, that is 0.06 rad — ~50× less than an MZM.
  • The shift uses n_g (dispersion moves the resonance less than n_eff alone would predict). Typical depletion rings: 10–20 pm/V (lateral junction), up to ~40–50 pm/V (L-shaped/interleaved).

Modulations

  • On the ON/OFF figure, show where the laser sits (on the blue or red flank, ~half-linewidth detuned) — this bias point sets ER, IL and the sign of chirp.
  • For depletion (reverse bias) the resonance shifts red (n increases as carriers are removed).

  • Thermal stability: absorbed power heats the ring and red-shifts it; with the laser on the blue side the resonance moves away from the laser (negative feedback). On the red side → thermal bistability.

Design Considerations

Trade-offs and Optimization

Radius selection:

  • Smaller radius:
    • Larger FSR (easier WDM) Range of operation
    • Lower Q \(\Rightarrow\) Larger channel bandwidth
    • Higher bending loss (limits the power budget)
  • Larger radius:
    • Higher Q (smaller bandwidth)
    • Narrower resonance
    • Smaller FSR (reduced range of operation)

Optimal for modulators: \(r = 5-8 \mu m\)

  • Small r: large FSR (more WDM channels), lower C, but bend radiation loss grows rapidly below ~3–5 μm in 220 nm Si.
  • Note: Q does not depend directly on radius if losses are fixed per length — it is loss and coupling that set Q. Smaller rings have lower Q only because bend loss and stronger coupling per round trip dominate.

Coupling Gap Optimization

Gap vs. Coupling strength:

Speed Limitations

Bandwidth-Limiting Factors

1. Photon lifetime:

\[\tau_{photon} = \frac{Q}{\omega_0} = \frac{Q \lambda_0}{2\pi c}\]

Bandwidth:

\[f_{3dB,optical} = \frac{1}{2\pi \tau_{photon}} = \frac{c}{Q\lambda_0}\]

Example: Q = 10,000 at \(\lambda = 1.55 \mu m\)
→ \(f_{3dB} \approx 19\) GHz

  • Photon lifetime: energy decays with τ = Q/ω₀ → f ≈ c/(λQ). Q = 10k → ~19 GHz; check: correct.
  • Nuance for advanced students: a detuned ring can show peaking in its E-O response and exceed this naive limit (Sacher & Poon); the formula is a good first estimate.

Speed Limitations (continued)

RC limitation:

\[f_{3dB,electrical} = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}\]

Typical values:

  • Resistance: R = 50-100 Ω (driver + modulator)
  • Capacitance: C = 10-30 fF (depletion phase shifter)
  • Electrical bandwidth: 50-100 GHz

Total bandwidth:

\[\frac{1}{f_{3dB,total}^2} = \frac{1}{f_{3dB,optical}^2} + \frac{1}{f_{3dB,electrical}^2}\]

Usually photon lifetime limited for high-Q rings!

  • RC numbers: 50 Ω × 30 fF → 106 GHz; 100 Ω × 30 fF → 53 GHz; so 50–100+ GHz OK.
  • Root-sum-square combination is an approximation (cascade of two first-order poles).
  • Conclusion: rings are usually photon-lifetime limited; design Q for the data rate (rule: optical BW ≈ 0.7–1 × baud rate).

Extra: time-domain coupled-mode theory (CMT)

Treat the ring as one resonant mode with energy amplitude \(A(t)\) (\(|A|^2\) = stored energy), laser \(s_{in}\) at \(\omega_L\):

\[\frac{dA}{dt} = \left[j(\omega_0(t) - \omega_L) - \frac{1}{\tau}\right]A - j\mu\, s_{in}\]

\[s_{out} = s_{in} - j\mu A\]

  • \(\dfrac{1}{\tau} = \dfrac{1}{\tau_i} + \dfrac{1}{\tau_e}\), \(\tau_x = \dfrac{2Q_x}{\omega_0}\) (amplitude decay)
  • \(\mu^2 = 2/\tau_e\) (energy conservation)
  • Steady state reproduces the Lorentzian of the transfer-matrix model

Modulation enters through \(\omega_0(t)\): the voltage shifts the resonance, the field follows with time constant τ.

What CMT shows that the c/(λQ) rule does not:

  • Overshoot and ringing when the resonance moves faster than τ (beat between laser and resonance)
  • E-O response peaking for detuned lasers → bandwidth beyond the photon-lifetime limit (Sacher & Poon, Opt. Express 2008)
  • Large-signal eye diagrams in a few lines of Python (next slide)

Exact update for a time step Δt (coefficients constant within the step):

\(A_{n+1} = A_n e^{p\Delta t} - j\mu s\,\dfrac{e^{p\Delta t} - 1}{p}\), \(p = j\Delta\omega - 1/\tau\)

  • CMT is the standard tool for ring modulator dynamics; it needs only Q_i, Q_e, the shift per volt and the laser detuning.
  • Energy amplitude decays at 1/τ = ω₀/(2Q): energy decays at ω₀/Q, i.e. the photon lifetime τ_ph = Q/ω₀ of the previous slides.
  • The exact exponential update avoids the stiffness problem of a naive Euler step (optical frequencies are factored out).

Python: large-signal ring eye diagrams (CMT)

def ring_cmt(v, dt, Qi, Qe, eta, lam0=1550e-9, det=0.0):
    w0 = 2*np.pi*C0/lam0
    ti, te = 2*Qi/w0, 2*Qe/w0
    mu, wl = np.sqrt(2/te), 2*np.pi*C0/(lam0 + det)
    a, out = 0j, np.empty(len(v))
    for i, vi in enumerate(v):
        D = 2*np.pi*C0/(lam0 + eta*vi) - wl     # ω0(t) − ωL
        p = 1j*D - (1/ti + 1/te)
        e = np.exp(p*dt)
        a = a*e - 1j*mu*(e - 1)/p
        out[i] = abs(1 - 1j*mu*a)**2
    return out
  • Q_L ≈ 5k: fast, clean edges but small swing (low OMA).
  • Q_L ≈ 9k: best compromise at 25 Gb/s.
  • Q_L ≈ 20k: largest swing, but the field cannot follow → ringing and ISI.
  • The RC pole (100 Ω × 15 fF ≈ 106 GHz) is irrelevant: photon lifetime dominates.
  • This is the large-signal confirmation of the design rule Q ≈ c/(λ·0.7·bit rate): at 25 Gb/s that gives ~11k.
  • Notice the overshoot at Q ≈ 5k: on a fast edge the stored field interferes with the new detuning — it is the time-domain face of the response peaking.
  • Students can change the drive (pre-emphasis, PAM4 levels) and see the effect immediately: the function takes any voltage waveform.

Optimizing for Speed

Design Strategies

  1. Reduce Q (lower finesse):
    • Increase coupling \(\kappa\) (over-couple)
    • Trade extinction for speed
  2. Optimize phase shifter:
    • Minimize capacitance
    • Contacts close to the ring (low series R)
  3. Driver co-design:
    • Low output impedance
    • Pre-emphasis for high-Q rings
  4. Use multiple rings:
    • Cascaded configuration
    • Better extinction at high speed
  • Each strategy trades something: lower Q costs ER and efficiency (need more voltage).
  • A ~40 μm ring is electrically tiny (λ_RF ≈ mm): it is a lumped capacitor, so reduce R and C rather than using travelling-wave electrodes.
  • Pre-emphasis is the cheapest fix and widely used.

MRR Modulator: Eye Diagram Analysis

Performance Metrics

Key parameters:

  • Extinction Ratio (ER): \(ER = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_{ON}}{P_{OFF}}\right)\)

  • Insertion Loss (IL): \(IL = -10\log_{10}(T_{max})\)

  • Quality factor: \(Q = \frac{\lambda_0}{\Delta\lambda_{FWHM}}\)

For 25 Gbps NRZ:

  • Target ER: > 6 dB
  • Maximum IL: < 3 dB
  • Eye opening: > 65% of rail-to-rail

  • Define ER (P1/P0) and IL (loss at the ‘1’ level relative to no ring); with rings there is a trade-off controlled by laser detuning.
  • Note OMA (optical modulation amplitude) is what the receiver actually sees and is the better metric for link budgets.
  • Point to the eye diagrams: closure from limited bandwidth (ISI) vs noise.

Thermal Tuning

Wavelength Stabilization

Challenge: Fabrication variations cause resonance mismatch

Solution: Integrated heater for trimming

Thermo-optic tuning:

\(\Delta \lambda = \frac{\lambda_0}{n_g} \frac{dn_{eff}}{dT} \Delta T\) (group index, not \(n_{eff}\))

For silicon:

\(\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\Delta T} \approx 70 \text{ pm/K}\)

Typical tuning range needed: ±2-3 nm (±30-40°C)

Power: ~0.1–0.3 nm/mW with standard heaters → 10–30 mW per ring for ±2–3 nm; ~1–3 mW only with undercut

  • Δλ = λ·(dn_eff/dT)·ΔT / n_g: with dn_eff/dT ≈ 1.86e-4 and n_g ≈ 4.2 this gives ≈ 69 pm/K at 1550 (≈ 58 pm/K at 1310). Using n_eff ≈ 2.5 would wrongly give ~115 pm/K.
  • Fab variation: ±1 nm thickness → ~±1–2 nm resonance shift; that’s why ±2–3 nm range.
  • Tuning range must cover up to one FSR in the worst case (or use channel reassignment / barrel shifting) — heater power quickly dominates the link energy budget.

Coupled Microring Resonators

Double-Ring Configuration

Structure:

  • Two rings coupled to each other
  • Single bus waveguide or dual bus
  • Creates flat-top filter response

Transfer function:

More complex than single ring - requires coupled-mode theory

Advantages:

  • Flat passband (better for WDM)
  • Sharper roll-off
  • Higher extinction ratio
  • More fabrication tolerant
  • Two coupled resonators → supermodes split → flat-top, steeper roll-off (2nd-order filter, like a Butterworth).
  • ‘More fabrication tolerant’ only for the passband shape; the rings still must be mutually resonant → in practice needs a heater per ring.

Coupled Rings: Design

Design Parameters

Critical parameters:

  1. Inter-ring coupling: \(\kappa_{12}\)
  2. Bus-ring coupling: \(\kappa_1\), \(\kappa_2\)
  3. Ring circumferences: Can be identical or slightly different

Design guidelines:

  • \(\kappa_{12} \approx \kappa_{bus}^2/4\) (power coupling, maximally flat, Little et al. 1997)
  • Requires careful optimization
  • More sensitive to fabrication variations

Performance:

  • Flattened passband: a few % of FSR
  • Extinction: >10 dB
  • Insertion loss: <2 dB
  • The inter-ring coupling must be much weaker than the bus coupling: κ₁₂ ≈ κ_bus²/4 for a maximally flat (Butterworth) 2nd-order response (Little et al., JLT 1997). Too strong → split (double-peaked) passband.
  • Passband width of a 2nd-order filter is a few % of the FSR; higher order (3–5 rings) to get box-like responses.

Cascaded Ring Modulators

Series Configuration

Concept:

  • Multiple rings in series along bus waveguide
  • Each ring slightly detuned or identical
  • Multiplicative extinction ratio

Extinction improvement:

\(ER_{total} = ER_1 + ER_2 + ... + ER_N \text{ [dB]}\)

Example:

  • 3 rings, each ER = 6 dB
  • Total ER ≈ 18 dB

Trade-off: Increased insertion loss

  • ER adds in dB only if all rings act on the same wavelength in the OFF state and are perfectly aligned; in practice alignment is hard.
  • IL also adds in dB in the ON state → ER/IL trade-off.
  • ‘Multiplicative ER’ in linear = additive in dB (same statement).

Vernier Effect Rings

Wide Tuning Range

Concept:

  • Two rings with slightly different FSR
  • Resonances align periodically
  • Effective tuning range = LCM of FSRs

FSR relationship:

\(FSR_1 = FSR_{ref}\) \(FSR_2 = FSR_{ref}(1 + \delta)\)

Where \(\delta\) = 0.01-0.05 (1-5% difference)

Tuning range:

\(\Delta\lambda_{tuning} \approx \frac{FSR_{ref}}{\delta}\)

Application: Tunable lasers, widely tunable filters

  • Vernier: extended FSR = FSR₁·FSR₂/|FSR₁ − FSR₂| ≈ FSR/δ — consistent with the slide.
  • Tuning one ring by one small FSR difference moves the coincidence by a whole FSR → ‘Vernier gain’ 1/δ.
  • ‘LCM of FSRs’ is loose wording; the formula above is precise.

Python: Vernier rings

def ring_adddrop(phi, a, t1, t2):
    den = 1 - 2*a*t1*t2*np.cos(phi) + (a*t1*t2)**2
    thr = (t2**2*a**2 - 2*a*t1*t2*np.cos(phi) + t1**2) / den
    drop = (1 - t1**2)*(1 - t2**2)*a / den
    return thr, drop

D1 = ring_adddrop(ring_phase(lam, 1550e-9, 10e-6), a, t, t)[1]
D2 = ring_adddrop(ring_phase(lam, 1550e-9, 10.8e-6), a, t, t)[1]
total = D1 * D2                     # cascaded filters
  • \(FSR_V = \dfrac{FSR_1\,FSR_2}{|FSR_1 - FSR_2|}\) = 9.10 × 8.43 / 0.67 ≈ 114 nm
  • Side modes at the neighbouring near-coincidences: their suppression sets the side-mode suppression of a Vernier laser.
  • Heating one ring by one small FSR difference (0.67 nm) moves the coincidence by a whole FSR (9 nm): Vernier gain ≈ 13.
  • The same structure is the wavelength selector of most widely tunable Si/III-V lasers (Module 5).
  • Ask: why not make the FSRs even closer? → larger FSR_V but the neighbouring coincidences are almost as good as the main one: poor side-mode suppression. Design trade-off between range and selectivity.

Higher-Order Modulation: PAM4

Pulse Amplitude Modulation

PAM4 basics:

  • 4 amplitude levels (2 bits per symbol)
  • Doubles data rate for same bandwidth
  • Widely used in data centers (400G, 800G)

Signal levels:

  • Level 0: 0 (00)
  • Level 1: V/3 (01)
  • Level 2: 2V/3 (11)
  • Level 3: V (10)

(Gray coding: neighbours differ by one bit)

Advantage: 50 Gbps with 25 GHz bandwidth

  • PAM4: 2 bits/symbol, 3 eyes, each eye 1/3 the amplitude → ~9.5 dB SNR penalty vs NRZ at the same swing.
  • Gray coding (00, 01, 11, 10) is the IEEE 802.3 mapping: a symbol error to an adjacent level costs only one bit error. With plain binary, the middle transition 01↔︎10 would cost two.
  • Equally spaced voltage levels don’t give equally spaced optical levels with a Lorentzian — link to the linearity slide.

PAM4 with MRR Modulators

Implementation Approaches

Method 1: Single ring with multi-level drive

  • Drive ring with 4-level electrical signal
  • Requires very linear modulator
  • Simple architecture

Method 2: Segmented electrode

  • Multiple phase shifters in ring
  • Binary-weighted control
  • Better linearity

Method 3: Multiple rings

  • Cascaded rings with binary weighting
  • Best linearity
  • More complex design
  • Method 2 = optical DAC: segments driven by the MSB/LSB with NRZ drivers (2:1 length ratio) — no linear driver needed.
  • Method 3 similar, with two rings in series.
  • These approaches move complexity from analog electronics to optics/layout.

PAM4: Linearity Requirements

Transfer Function Linearization

Challenge: MRR transfer function is Lorentzian (non-linear)

Solutions:

  1. Bias point optimization
    • Operate on linear portion of resonance
    • Use pre-distortion
  2. Dual-drive configuration
    • Push-pull operation
    • Improved linearity
  3. Digital pre-compensation
    • DSP-based equalization
    • Look-up table (LUT)
  4. Over-couple the ring
    • Reduces peak sharpness
    • More linear response
  • The Lorentzian slope is steepest at ~±FWHM/(2√3) detuning — bias there for best linearity.
  • Over-coupling flattens the response and improves linearity but costs efficiency.
  • LUT/pre-distortion is what commercial PAM4 drivers do (level adjust per eye).

Python: PAM4 levels with a ring

lin = np.arange(4) * Vpp / 3                 # 0, V/3, 2V/3, V
Tl = np.array([T(v) for v in lin])           # optical levels
target = np.linspace(Tl[0], Tl[-1], 4)       # equal spacing
m = (V >= 0) & (V <= Vpp)
pre = np.interp(target, TV[m], V[m])         # invert T(V): LUT
RLM = 3 * np.diff(Tl).min() / (Tl[-1] - Tl[0])
  • The smallest eye sets the BER: RLM < 1 is a direct SNR penalty.
  • IEEE 802.3 requires RLM ≥ 0.95 at the transmitter.
  • The inverse-LUT is exactly what a PAM4 driver’s per-level adjust (or the DSP) implements.
  • The Lorentzian is steepest near ±FWHM/(2√3); biasing there makes the middle eye the largest and compresses the outer ones.
  • With a DAC driver the pre-distortion costs nothing; with an “optical DAC” (next extra slide) the segment lengths must be chosen unequal to get the same effect.

Extra: optical DAC – segmented ring for PAM4

Idea: replace the linear PAM4 driver (DAC + linear amplifier) by two NRZ drivers acting on two phase-shifter segments of the same ring:

  • MSB drives a segment of length \(L_{MSB}\), LSB a segment \(L_{LSB}\)
  • Resonance shift: \(\Delta\lambda = \eta'(L_{MSB}b_1 + L_{LSB}b_0)V\)
  • Equal shifts in λ → \(L_{MSB} = 2L_{LSB}\)
  • Equal optical levels → adjust the ratio (≠ 2) to compensate the Lorentzian, as on the previous slide

Advantages: NRZ CMOS drivers (inverters) are simple, fast and energy efficient; no linearity requirement on the electronics.

Costs and limits:

  • The level spacing is fixed at layout → only fine-tuning with the bias/laser detuning
  • Two RF inputs per ring, two contact pairs inside a small ring
  • Timing skew between MSB and LSB closes the eye

Same idea for MZMs: segmented MZM with binary-weighted segments driven by NRZ inverters (a “photonic DAC”) — popular in monolithic CMOS-photonics.

Rule of thumb: move the complexity where it is cheapest — DSP for long-reach links, optics/layout for low-power short links.

  • This is how several co-packaged-optics demonstrations generate PAM4 with CMOS drivers.
  • Ask students to compute the segment ratio that gives equal optical levels using the Python model (vary the LSB fraction until RLM ≈ 1).

Driver Circuits

Common Architectures

1. Lumped driver

  • Simple resistor-capacitor model
  • Suitable for slow modulators
  • Not ideal for >25 Gbps

2. Segmented driver

  • Multiple driver stages
  • Distributed along modulator
  • Better for longer modulators

3. Traveling-wave driver

  • Velocity matching
  • Ultra-broadband (>100 GHz)
  • Complex design
  • Lumped: device ≪ RF wavelength (rule of thumb L < λ_RF/10; at 50 GHz in Si λ_RF ≈ 1.5–2 mm → L < ~150 μm). Rings always qualify.
  • Segmented: lumped segments each with their own small driver (distributed driver) — popular in co-integrated MZMs.
  • Traveling-wave: for long MZMs (part E).

Driver: Lumped Model

Equivalent Circuit

Basic lumped model:

Driver (R_d) → Transmission line → Modulator (C_mod, R_mod)

Transfer function:

\(H(f) = \frac{1}{1 + j2\pi f RC}\)

3-dB bandwidth:

\(f_{3dB} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_{total} C_{mod}}\)

Where \(R_{total} = R_d + R_{mod} + R_{line}\)

  • R_total = R_driver + R_contact + R_Si; the doped-silicon series resistance often dominates for rings.
  • If the transmission line is matched, it doesn’t add R; it adds delay. The model is valid only if the load is lumped.

Driver Impedance Matching

Maximizing Power Transfer

Impedance matching:

\(R_{driver} = 50\, \Omega \text{ (standard)}\)

Termination schemes:

  1. Series termination at driver
    • \(R_{term} = 50 - R_{driver}\)
  2. Parallel termination at load
    • \(R_{term} = 50\, \Omega\) to ground
  3. Matched termination
    • Both series and parallel

For low capacitance modulators:

  • Depletion MRR: C ≈ 10-20 fF
  • Can operate without termination
  • RC bandwidth > 100 GHz
  • For an unterminated small capacitive load at the end of a 50 Ω line, the voltage doubles (open-circuit reflection) → you get 2× swing for free, but reflections must be absorbed at the source (series/back termination).
  • Parallel 50 Ω termination wastes power (V²/50) — avoided for rings.

Bandwidth Optimization

Design Strategies

1. Reduce capacitance:

  • Shorter phase shifter
  • Higher doping (trade-off with efficiency)
  • Optimized junction geometry

2. Reduce resistance:

  • Low-resistance contacts (silicide)
  • Wide metal traces
  • Multiple contact points

3. Over-couple the ring:

  • Lower Q → higher optical bandwidth
  • Trade extinction for speed

4. Pre-emphasis:

  • Boost high frequencies electrically
  • Compensate for roll-off
  • Higher doping lowers R (and increases C, but improves efficiency) — reword the slide line ‘Higher doping (trade-off with efficiency)’ → ‘trade-off with loss’.
  • Silicide contacts and P++/N++ close to the rib reduce R but add optical loss.

Pre-Emphasis and Equalization

Signal Processing Techniques

Pre-emphasis:

\(V_{out}(f) = V_{in}(f) \cdot H_{pre}(f)\)

Where \(H_{pre}(f) = 1 + \alpha \cdot \frac{jf}{f_0}\)

Feed-Forward Equalization (FFE):

  • Multi-tap FIR filter
  • Compensates for ISI (inter-symbol interference)
  • Typically 3-7 taps

Decision Feedback Equalization (DFE):

  • Corrects based on previous decisions
  • Removes post-cursor ISI
  • Used in receivers primarily

  • Pre-emphasis (Tx FFE) boosts transitions; the ring’s own low-pass (photon lifetime) is compensated.
  • H_pre = 1 + α·jf/f₀ is a zero; real implementations are 2–3 tap FIRs.
  • DFE is in the receiver because it needs decisions.

Pre-emphasis and linear equalization

  • Compare the eyes: pre-emphasis opens the vertical eye but adds overshoot; linear equalization boosts noise too (noise enhancement).

  • Causes wavelength drift
  • Requires careful thermal isolation

Power Consumption Analysis

Energy Efficiency

Single MRR modulator:

\(E_{bit} = \frac{P_{total}}{Data\,Rate}\)

Power components:

  1. Dynamic (switching): \(P_{dyn} = \frac{1}{2} C V^2 f\); for random NRZ \(E_{bit} = CV^2/4\)
  2. Static (leakage): Minimal for depletion
  3. Thermal tuning: 1–30 mW per ring
  4. Driver: 1–10 mW (co-integrated CMOS) to 100–200 mW (50 Ω external)

Example: 50G PAM4

  • C = 15 fF, V = 2.5 V, f = 25 GHz
  • \(P_{dyn}\) ≈ 1.2 mW → ≈ 23 fJ/bit (modulator only)

E. Mach-Zehnder Modulators

  • Section E: interference-based modulation — broadband, high ER, chirp control, but long and power-hungry drivers.

Introduction to MZM

Operating Principles

Concept:

  • Split optical signal into two arms
  • Apply differential phase shift
  • Interfere at output
  • Amplitude modulation via interference

Advantages:

  • Broadband operation
  • High extinction ratio
  • Chirp management
  • Mature technology

Key equation:

\(P_{out} = P_{in} \cos^2\left(\frac{\Delta\phi}{2}\right)\)

  • Two arms, two 3-dB couplers: phase difference → intensity via cos².
  • Broadband: no resonance → no thermal locking of the modulator itself (only bias control).
  • Ask: which arm loss imbalance limits ER? (unequal splitting/arm loss)

MZM: Transfer Function

Interference-Based Modulation

Phase difference:

\(\Delta\phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta n_{eff} L\)

Output power:

\(P_{out} = \frac{P_{in}}{2}[1 + \cos(\Delta\phi)]\)

For push-pull operation:

\(\Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\Delta n_1 - \Delta n_2) L\)

Effectively doubles the phase shift efficiency!

  • Show the equivalence: cos²(Δφ/2) = ½[1+cos Δφ].
  • Push-pull: Δn₁ = −Δn₂ → each arm needs only half the phase.
  • Note: with depletion, both arms get opposite bias changes around a DC reverse bias, not truly ±V on the junction.

MZM Bias Points

Operating Regimes

Bias conditions:

  • Null point (\(\Delta\phi = \pi\)): \(P_{out} = 0\)
  • Quadrature (\(\Delta\phi = \pi/2\)): \(P_{out} = P_{in}/2\)
  • Peak (\(\Delta\phi = 0, 2\pi\)): \(P_{out} = P_{in}\)

Modulation:

  • Push-pull at quadrature: intensity OOK / PAM4, chirp-free
  • Single-arm at quadrature: simplest, linear for analog, but chirped
  • Push-pull at null: field modulation (BPSK, I/Q arms of an IQ modulator)
  • Intensity OOK/PAM: quadrature, where the intensity slope is maximum. At null the field changes sign (±E): used for BPSK and in each arm of an IQ modulator; driven around null the intensity would frequency-double.
  • Single-arm drive at quadrature → linear but chirped; dual-arm push-pull → chirp-free.

Python: MZM transfer, swing and chirp

def mzm_field(phi1, phi2):

\(V_\pi\) and \(V_\pi.L\)

Key Performance Metric

Definition:

\(V_\pi\) = Voltage required for \(\pi\) phase shift

\(V_\pi = \frac{\lambda}{2 L \Delta n_{eff}/\Delta V}\)

Half-wave voltage:

For push-pull MZM:

\(V_{\pi,pp} = \frac{V_\pi}{2}\)

Figure of merit:

\(V_\pi L = V_\pi \times L\)

Typical values:

  • Carrier injection: 0.01–0.05 V·cm (but slow)
  • Carrier depletion: 1.5–3 V·cm (lateral), ~1 V·cm (L/U-shaped)
  • Thin-film LiNbO₃: ~2 V·cm (Pockels, >100 GHz)
  • V_π: voltage for Δφ = π; V_πL is the length-independent FOM.
  • Push-pull: each arm needs half the swing; the differential swing equals the single-arm V_π. Datasheets differ on which one they quote — fix one convention for the course.
  • Values consistent with Section B: injection is the most efficient per volt, depletion is the one used for data.
  • Depletion V_πL also varies with bias (C(V) nonlinear) — quote the bias.

Differential Drive

Implementation:

  • Negative voltage on other arm: -V

Advantages:

  • 2× efficiency: Half the voltage for same ER
  • Chirp control: Symmetric intensity modulation
  • Common-mode rejection: Noise immunity
  • Better linearity: Balanced operation

  • Differential output
  • 180° phase relationship
  • Matched impedance on both arms

Chirp in MZM

Frequency Modulation

Chirp parameter:

\(\alpha = \frac{\Delta\phi_1 + \Delta\phi_2}{\Delta\phi_1 - \Delta\phi_2}\)

Types:

  • \(\alpha = 0\): Zero chirp (ideal push-pull)
  • \(\alpha > 0\): Positive chirp (blue-shift on rising edge)
  • \(\alpha < 0\): Negative chirp (red-shift on rising edge)

Impact:

  • Dispersion penalty in fiber
  • Spectral broadening
  • System reach limitation

Control: Adjust relative drive on two arms

  • Chirp: the output field acquires a time-varying phase → instantaneous frequency shift.
  • α_chirp = (Δφ₁+Δφ₂)/(Δφ₁−Δφ₂) (small-signal): zero in perfect push-pull, ±1 for single-arm drive.
  • In fiber: positive α with standard SMF at 1550 enhances dispersion penalty; slight negative chirp can extend reach.
  • In Si there is also amplitude–phase coupling via free-carrier absorption (Δα), so even push-pull can have residual chirp.
  • Note: the previous speaker notes on this slide described crossover distortion in push-pull electronic amplifiers — unrelated to optical chirp; replaced.

Extra: the chirp of a Mach-Zehnder modulator

Output field (ideal 50:50 couplers, arm phases \(\phi_1, \phi_2\)):

\[E_{out} = \frac{E_{in}}{2}\left(e^{j\phi_1} + e^{j\phi_2}\right) = E_{in}\,e^{j\frac{\phi_1+\phi_2}{2}}\cos\frac{\phi_1 - \phi_2}{2}\]

  • Intensity depends only on the difference \(\phi_1 - \phi_2\)
  • Output phase \(\Phi = (\phi_1 + \phi_2)/2\) → instantaneous frequency \(\Delta f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\)

Chirp (Henry) factor \(\alpha = \dfrac{d\Phi/dt}{\frac{1}{2I}dI/dt}\):

\[\alpha = -\frac{\dot\phi_1 + \dot\phi_2}{\dot\phi_1 - \dot\phi_2}\cot\frac{\phi_1 - \phi_2}{2}\]

Cases (at quadrature, \(\cot(\pi/4) = 1\)):

Drive \(\dot\phi_2/\dot\phi_1\) \(|\alpha|\)
Single arm 0 1
Push-pull −1 0
60/40 push-pull −2/3 0.2

In silicon the arms also change loss with voltage (free-carrier absorption), which unbalances the phasors: even perfect push-pull has a small residual chirp.

Why it matters: in dispersive fiber (C-band SMF, 17 ps/nm/km) chirp converts to intensity distortion; a slightly negative α can extend the reach of NRZ links.

  • Derivation is one line (factor out the average phase). Students then see immediately why push-pull is chirp-free.
  • In O-band datacom links (near zero dispersion) chirp is rarely a limit; in C-band it is.

Traveling-Wave MZM

Velocity Matching

Concept:

  • Distribute electrodes along full length
  • Match RF velocity to optical velocity
  • Ultra-broadband operation

Design:

\(v_{RF} = v_{optical}\)

\(v_{RF} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\epsilon_{eff,RF}}}\)

\(v_{optical} = \frac{c}{n_g}\)

Requires: \(\sqrt{\epsilon_{eff,RF}} = n_g\)

In silicon: \(n_g \approx 3.9\)–\(4.2\); an unloaded CPW has \(n_{RF} \approx 2\)–\(2.6\), but the PN-junction capacitance loads the line → \(n_{RF} \approx 3.5\)–\(4\), \(Z \approx 25\)–\(40\ \Omega\). Matching is achievable; RF loss is the main limit.

  • TW: RF and optical waves co-propagate; the bandwidth is set by velocity mismatch, RF loss and impedance mismatch, not RC.
  • Loaded line: n_RF = c·√(L′(C′_line + C′_j)), Z = √(L′/(C′_line + C′_j)). Adding junction capacitance raises n_RF and lowers Z simultaneously — designers use the segment fill factor to trade the two.

Traveling-Wave Design

Bandwidth Extension

Conventional limitations:

  • RC limited to 40-60 GHz
  • Mismatch causes reflection

TW-MZM advantages:

  • Bandwidth >100 GHz possible
  • Constant impedance
  • No reflections

Implementation:

  • Coplanar waveguide (CPW) electrode
  • Slow-wave structures
  • Careful impedance control (50 Ω)
  • Bandwidth limit for velocity mismatch: f₃dB ≈ 1.4c/(π·L·|n_g − n_RF|).
  • ‘Constant impedance, no reflections’ only if terminated with Z₀ (on-chip or external termination) → termination dissipates V²/(2Z₀) power.
  • Slow-wave (T-rail, capacitively loaded) electrodes are the standard trick to tune n_RF and Z.

Extra: travelling-wave response derivation

RF voltage on a matched line: \(V(z,t) = V_0 e^{-\alpha z}\cos\left[\omega\left(t - \frac{n_{RF}z}{c}\right)\right]\)

A photon entering at \(t_0\) is at \(z\) at time \(t_0 + n_g z/c\) and sees

\[V = V_0 e^{-\alpha z}\cos\left[\omega t_0 + \omega\frac{(n_g - n_{RF})z}{c}\right]\]

Integrating the phase along \(L\) and normalising:

\[H(f) = \frac{1 - e^{-(\alpha + j\Delta\beta)L}}{(\alpha + j\Delta\beta)L},\qquad \Delta\beta = \frac{2\pi f (n_g - n_{RF})}{c}\]

Two limits (−3 dB electrical, \(|H| = 1/\sqrt{2}\)):

  • Lossless, mismatched: \(|H| = |\mathrm{sinc}(\Delta\beta L/2)|\) → \[f_{3dB} \approx \frac{1.39\,c}{\pi L |n_g - n_{RF}|}\]
  • Matched, lossy: \(|H| = (1 - e^{-\alpha L})/\alpha L\) → bandwidth reached when the total RF loss is αL ≈ 0.74 Np = 6.4 dB

Si numbers: RF loss ~3 dB/(cm·√GHz) → 6.4 dB at 2 mm is reached near 50 GHz; add the junction’s own RC pole (~60 GHz) → ≈ 35 GHz.

Also: impedance mismatch at the input and the termination (reflections → ripple).

  • The “6.4 dB rule” is the most useful single number for TW design: compute the RF loss of your line and find where it reaches 6.4 dB.
  • In Si, velocity mismatch is usually secondary (loaded n_RF ≈ 3.6–4 vs n_g ≈ 3.9–4.2); the doped-silicon RF loss dominates.
  • The junction pole: each loaded segment is an RC (slab resistance × junction C), independent of length.

Python Implementation

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class PNJunction:
    def __init__(self, N_A, N_D, T=300):
        """Initialize PN junction parameters"""
        self.N_A = N_A  # Acceptor concentration (cm^-3)
        self.N_D = N_D  # Donor concentration (cm^-3)
        self.T = T      # Temperature (K)
        
        # Physical constants
        self.q = 1.6e-19      # Elementary charge (C)
        self.k = 1.38e-23     # Boltzmann constant (J/K)
        self.eps_0 = 8.854e-14  # Vacuum permittivity (F/cm)
        self.eps_si = 11.7 * self.eps_0  # Silicon permittivity
        self.n_i = 1.45e10    # Intrinsic carrier concentration
        
        # Calculate built-in potential
        self.V_bi = (self.k * self.T / self.q) * \
                    np.log(self.N_A * self.N_D / self.n_i**2)
    
    def depletion_width(self, V=0):
        """Calculate depletion width for given bias voltage"""
        factor = 2 * self.eps_si / self.q
        doping_term = (self.N_A + self.N_D) / (self.N_A * self.N_D)
        W = np.sqrt(factor * (self.V_bi - V) * doping_term)
        return W
    
    def capacitance(self, V, area=1e-8):
        """Calculate junction capacitance (F)"""
        W = self.depletion_width(V)
        C = self.eps_si * area / W
        return C
    
    def electric_field_max(self, V=0):
        """Calculate maximum electric field"""
        W = self.depletion_width(V)
        E_max = 2 * (self.V_bi - V) / W
        return E_max

Example Code: Phase Shifter Designer

Doping sweep with the lateral-PN model

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import erf

q, eps_s, VT, ni = 1.602e-19, 11.7 * 8.854e-14, 0.02585, 1.45e10

def depletion(NA, ND, V):                       # cm, V > 0 forward
    Vbi = VT * np.log(NA * ND / ni**2)
    W = np.sqrt(2 * eps_s / q * (Vbi - V) * (NA + ND) / (NA * ND))
    return W, W * ND / (NA + ND), W * NA / (NA + ND)

def depletion_ps(N, V, x0=0.0, w0=0.28e-4, gy=0.8):
    # Δn_eff (vs 0 V) and loss (dB/cm) of a lateral PN, Soref 1550 nm
    ov = lambda a, b: 0.5 * (erf(np.sqrt(2) * b / w0) - erf(np.sqrt(2) * a / w0))
    _, xp0, xn0 = depletion(N, N, 0.0)
    _, xp, xn = depletion(N, N, V)
    dn = gy * (8.8e-22 * N * ov(x0 + xn0, x0 + xn) + 8.5e-18 * N**0.8 * ov(x0 - xp, x0 - xp0))
    alpha = gy * (8.5e-18 * N * ov(x0 + xn, np.inf) + 6.0e-18 * N * ov(-np.inf, x0 - xp))
    return dn, 4.343 * alpha

def design(N, Vpp=2.0, L=0.2, lam=1.55e-4):    # L in cm
    dn, _ = depletion_ps(N, -Vpp)
    _, loss = depletion_ps(N, -Vpp / 2)
    VpiL = Vpp * lam / (2 * dn)
    Cmm = eps_s * 0.22e-4 / depletion(N, N, -Vpp / 2)[0] * 0.1 * 1e15
    return VpiL, loss, VpiL / L, loss * L, Cmm

print(" N (cm^-3)  VpiL(V.cm)  loss(dB/cm)  Vpi(V)  IL(dB)  C'(fF/mm)")
for N in np.logspace(17, 18.3, 8):
    print(f"{N:9.2e}  " + "  ".join(f"{v:9.2f}" for v in design(N)))

Key Equations Summary

Essential Formulas

PN Junction: \(V_{bi} = \frac{kT}{q}\ln\left(\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}\right), \quad W = \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_s(V_{bi}-V)}{q}\frac{N_A+N_D}{N_A N_D}}\)

Plasma Dispersion: \(\Delta n = -[8.8\times10^{-22}\Delta N_e + 8.5\times10^{-18}(\Delta N_h)^{0.8}]\)

Phase Shift: \(\Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta n_{eff} L\)

MRR Transfer: \(T = \left|\frac{t-ae^{j\phi}}{1-ate^{j\phi}}\right|^2\)

MZM Output: \(P_{out} = \frac{P_{in}}{2}[1+\cos(\Delta\phi)]\)

Bandwidth: \(f_{3dB} = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}\)

Backup: Carrier Injection Details

Forward Bias Analysis

Injection efficiency:

\(\eta_{inj} = \frac{I_{minority}}{I_{total}}\)

Minority carrier distribution:

\(\Delta n(x) = \Delta n(0) e^{-x/L_d}\)

Where \(L_d = \sqrt{D\tau}\) is the diffusion length.

For typical Si parameters:

  • \(L_d \approx 50\) μm (holes in n-region)
  • \(\tau \approx 1\) μs
  • Need short injection region for fast response
  • Diffusion length L_d = √(Dτ): holes D ≈ 12 cm²/s, τ = 1 μs → ~35 μm, consistent with ~50 μm order.
  • In SOI waveguides τ is ~ns (surface recombination) → L_d ~ 1 μm: carriers stay near the intrinsic region, which is good for efficiency.
