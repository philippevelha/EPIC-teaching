Introduction to Circuit Modeling

Why Circuit-Level Simulation?

Challenges with full-physics:

  • FDTD/FEM too slow for large circuits
  • Cannot simulate >100 components
  • Impractical for system-level design
  • No statistical analysis capability

Benefits of compact models:

  • Fast simulation (seconds vs hours)
  • Enable system-level design
  • Statistical/Monte Carlo analysis
  • Co-simulation with electronics

Comparison between FDTD simulation and Compact model of a waveguide

  • Order of magnitude: a 3D FDTD of a 10 µm ring takes minutes to hours; a compact model of the same ring is a formula evaluated in microseconds. A 1000-component circuit would be impossible in FDTD and is trivial with compact models.
  • The figure shows that a well-built compact model reproduces the FDTD result within the accuracy of the fabrication itself, which is the only accuracy that matters.
  • Key message: physics simulation is used once per component (in the PDK), circuit simulation is used every time a designer changes something.

Modeling Hierarchy

Multi-Level Approach

Level 1: Physical simulation

  • FDTD, FEM, EME
  • Accurate but slow
  • Used for component design
  • Generates compact model data

Level 2: Compact models

  • Analytical or fitted equations
  • S-parameters or transfer matrices
  • Fast evaluation
  • Core of circuit simulation

Level 3: System models

  • Behavioral descriptions
  • Include noise, nonlinearity
  • Link budget analysis
  • Performance prediction
  • Analogy with electronics: Level 1 is TCAD (device physics), Level 2 is the SPICE transistor model (BSIM), Level 3 is behavioural Verilog-A / system blocks.
  • Each level is calibrated on the level below and validated against measurements. Errors propagate upwards, so validation of Level 2 against measured test structures is essential.

Compact Model Requirements

Essential Characteristics

Accuracy:

  • Match physical simulation within tolerance
  • Valid over operating range
  • Include fabrication variations
  • Capture wavelength dependence

Speed:

  • Evaluation time << 1 ms
  • Enable Monte Carlo simulation
  • Support optimization loops
  • Real-time design iteration

Completeness:

  • All relevant parameters included
  • Temperature dependence
  • Nonlinear effects when important
  • Proper causality

Usability:

  • Clear parameter definitions
  • Documentation included
  • Error handling
  • Standard interfaces
  • “Proper causality” and “passivity” are often forgotten: a fitted model can predict |S21| > 1 or a non-causal impulse response, which breaks time-domain simulation (see the extra slide on Kramers–Kronig).
  • “Standard interfaces”: ports with names, units (µm, nm, V, K), and an S-matrix convention (sign of the phase, e^{-jβL} vs e^{+jβL}). Mixing conventions between two vendors’ models is a classic bug.

S-Parameter Representation

Network Analysis Approach

S-matrix formulation:

\[\begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & \cdots & S_{1n} \\ S_{21} & S_{22} & \cdots & S_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ S_{n1} & S_{n2} & \cdots & S_{nn} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{bmatrix}\]

Where:

  • \(a_i\) = incident wave at port i
  • \(b_i\) = reflected/transmitted wave at port i
  • \(S_{ij}\) = transmission from port j to port i

Properties:

  • Frequency dependent: \(S_{ij}(f, \lambda)\)
  • Complex-valued (magnitude and phase)
  • Reciprocal for passive devices: \(S_{ij} = S_{ji}\)
  • Lossless: \(S^\dagger S = I\) (unitary); lossy: \(I - S^\dagger S \succeq 0\)
  • Normalise the waves so that |a_i|² is the power entering port i; then |S_ij|² is a power transmission.
  • In photonics a “port” is usually one mode of one waveguide. A waveguide carrying TE0 and TM0 has two ports at each end: polarisation is just more ports.
  • Reciprocity holds for any linear device without magneto-optic material or time modulation (Module 5, isolators). Unitarity is the energy conservation check to run on every fitted model.

Extra: what reciprocity and unitarity buy you

Optional deep-dive.

Lossless reciprocal 2×2 coupler

\(S\) symmetric and unitary ⇒ with \(S = \begin{bmatrix} t & \kappa' \\ \kappa' & t\end{bmatrix}\) (transmission block):

\[|t|^2 + |\kappa'|^2 = 1, \qquad t^*\kappa' + \kappa'^* t = 0\]

The second condition forces a 90° phase between bar and cross: \(\kappa' = \pm j\,|\kappa'|\) when \(t\) is real. This is why the coupler matrix contains \(j\kappa\).

Consequence: a balanced MZI with two 50:50 couplers sends all the light to the cross port.

Checks to run on any S-matrix (Python):

import numpy as np
def check_smatrix(S, tol=1e-6):
    """S: (n_wl, n, n) complex array."""
    recip = np.max(np.abs(S - S.transpose(0, 2, 1)))
    # largest eigenvalue of S^H S must be <= 1 (passivity)
    ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.conj(S.transpose(0, 2, 1)) @ S)
    return {"reciprocity error": recip,
            "max gain (passivity <= 1)": ev.max()}

A fitted model with a gain > 1 at some wavelength will make a ring “lase” in a circuit simulation.

  • Derivation on the board: write S^H S = I for the 2×2 transmission block, the off-diagonal term gives tκ’ + κ’t = 0, so Re(t*κ’) = 0.
  • The 90° phase is physical: it is the phase difference between the even and odd supermodes at the coupler output.
  • The Python check is worth running on any S-parameter file received from a foundry or generated by a fit.

Transfer Matrix Method

Cascaded Component Analysis

Transfer matrix (T-matrix):

\[\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ b_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} T_{11} & T_{12} \\ T_{21} & T_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b_2 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix}\]

Cascade rule:

\[[T_{total}] = [T_1][T_2][T_3] \cdots [T_n]\]

Advantages:

  • Simple cascading of components
  • Intuitive for linear chains
  • Easy conversion from S-parameters

Limitation: only for chains of 2-port (or 2N-port) blocks; feedback loops, rings with several couplers, or arbitrary topologies need the S-matrix netlist approach (next slides).

Conversion S → T (port 1 on the left, port 2 on the right):

\[T = \frac{1}{S_{21}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -S_{22} \\ S_{11} & -\Delta_S \end{bmatrix}\]

where \(\Delta_S = S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}S_{21}\)

  • The T-matrix relates the waves on the left of the block to those on the right, so blocks multiply in the order they appear.
  • Conventions differ between textbooks (which wave is on top); check consistency with a trivial case: a lossless waveguide must give T = diag(e^{jφ}, e^{-jφ}).
  • T-matrices are numerically unstable for long chains with strong evanescent/lossy sections (exponentials blow up); S-matrix cascading (Redheffer star product) is the stable alternative.

Extra: S ↔︎ T derivation and the star product

Optional deep-dive.

Start from \(b_1 = S_{11}a_1 + S_{12}a_2\), \(b_2 = S_{21}a_1 + S_{22}a_2\).

Solve the second for \(a_1\):

\[a_1 = \frac{1}{S_{21}}\,b_2 - \frac{S_{22}}{S_{21}}\,a_2\]

Insert in the first:

\[b_1 = \frac{S_{11}}{S_{21}}\,b_2 - \frac{\Delta_S}{S_{21}}\,a_2\]

which gives the T-matrix of the previous slide. Note the division by \(S_{21}\): a block that does not transmit (S₂₁ → 0) has no T-matrix.

Redheffer star product (cascading two S-matrices A then B directly):

\[S_{11} = A_{11} + A_{12}B_{11}(I - A_{22}B_{11})^{-1}A_{21}\] \[S_{21} = B_{21}(I - A_{22}B_{11})^{-1}A_{21}\]

The factor \((I - A_{22}B_{11})^{-1}\) is the sum of all multiple reflections between A and B: \(I + A_{22}B_{11} + (A_{22}B_{11})^2 + \dots\)

This is exactly the structure of the general netlist solver: \((I - C\,S_{ii})^{-1}\).

  • The geometric series interpretation makes the formulas intuitive: every round trip between the two blocks adds one more term.
  • Use this to motivate the netlist solver: the same inverse appears, but for an arbitrary connection matrix instead of a single interface.

Compact Model Types: Classification of Models

1. Analytical models:

  • Based on closed-form equations
  • Derived from physics
  • Fast evaluation
  • Limited accuracy for complex structures

Example - Waveguide: \[n_{eff}(\lambda, w, h) = f(\text{geometry, material})\]

2. Fitted models:

  • Polynomial or spline fits
  • Based on simulation data
  • Better accuracy
  • Require fitting database

3. Lookup table (LUT):

  • Interpolation from database
  • Highest accuracy possible
  • Larger memory footprint
  • Slower than analytical

4. Behavioral models:

  • High-level description
  • System-level parameters only
  • Very fast
  • Lower fidelity

Example - MRR: \[T(\lambda) = \text{Lorentzian}(Q, \lambda_0, \kappa)\]

  • In practice PDK models are hybrids: an analytical structure (e.g. ring = coupler + waveguide) whose parameters (κ, n_eff, n_g, loss) are fitted or tabulated versus geometry.
  • The Python yield example later uses a 2D LUT n_eff(w, h) built from the EIM: a LUT model in ten lines.
  • Behavioural Lorentzian models break when you change the context (e.g. coupling to a different bus), physical-structure models do not.

Waveguide Compact Model

Example: Strip Waveguide

Parameters: width \(w\), thickness \(h\), core/cladding indices, wavelength \(\lambda\), temperature \(T\)

Outputs used by every other model: \(n_{eff}\), \(n_g\), loss \(\alpha\), and from them the S-matrix

\[S_{21} = S_{12} = \exp\!\left(-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda} n_{eff}(\lambda) L - \frac{\alpha L}{2}\right)\]

Dispersion model (Taylor expansion around \(\lambda_0\)):

\[n_{eff}(\lambda) \approx n_0 - (n_g - n_0)\frac{\lambda-\lambda_0}{\lambda_0} + \tfrac{1}{2}D_2(\lambda-\lambda_0)^2\]

Only three numbers (\(n_0\), \(n_g\), \(D_2\)) describe the waveguide over the C-band.

Group index:

\[n_g = n_{eff} - \lambda \frac{dn_{eff}}{d\lambda}\]

Loss model:

\[\alpha(\lambda) = \alpha_{material} + \alpha_{scattering}(w, \sigma_{LER}) + \alpha_{bend}(R)\]

Scattering loss grows as the mode overlaps the sidewalls: narrow waveguides are lossier.

  • The waveguide S-matrix is the most important model of all: every interferometric device is “waveguides plus couplers”.
  • Show how the linear term in λ is fixed by n_g: n_g sets the FSR of rings and MZIs, n_eff sets where the resonances fall. Both are needed.
  • Loss in dB/cm converts to field attenuation α/2 in the exponent; a common error is to use the power coefficient in the field.

Python: building the waveguide model with the EIM

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

def slab_modes(ns, ds, lam, pol="TE"):
    """neff of guided modes of a multilayer slab
    ns = [n_left, n_1..n_k, n_right], ds = [d_1..d_k]"""
    k0 = 2*np.pi/lam
    p = (lambda n: 1) if pol == "TE" else (lambda n: 1/n**2)
    def f(neff):                      # boundary mismatch
        gl = k0*np.sqrt(neff**2 - ns[0]**2)
        gr = k0*np.sqrt(neff**2 - ns[-1]**2)
        v = np.array([1+0j, p(ns[0])*gl])   # (psi, p psi')
        for n, d in zip(ns[1:-1], ds):
            kx = k0*np.lib.scimath.sqrt(n**2 - neff**2)
            q = p(n)*kx
            c, s = np.cos(kx*d), np.sin(kx*d)
            v = np.array([c*v[0] + s/q*v[1], -q*s*v[0] + c*v[1]])
        return (v[1] + p(ns[-1])*gr*v[0]).real
    x = np.linspace(max(ns[0], ns[-1]) + 1e-7, max(ns) - 1e-7, 400)
    y = [f(xi) for xi in x]
    return sorted([brentq(f, x[i], x[i+1]) for i in range(399)
                   if np.sign(y[i]) != np.sign(y[i+1])], reverse=True)

def neff_strip(w, h=0.22, lam=1.55, nsi=3.476, nox=1.444):
    n_slab = slab_modes([nox, nsi, nox], [h], lam, "TE")[0]  # vertical
    return slab_modes([nox, n_slab, nox], [w], lam, "TM")[0]  # lateral
  • 500 × 220 nm: \(n_{eff}\) = 2.49, \(n_g\) = 4.02 (EIM). A full-vectorial mode solver gives ≈ 2.44 and ≈ 4.2: the EIM is a teaching model; a PDK uses FDE/FEM data.
  • Middle panel: inside the data range every fit is fine; outside, high-order polynomials explode.
  • The effective index method replaces the 2D cross-section by two 1D slab problems: first vertical (TE slab of 220 nm Si gives n_slab), then lateral with n_slab as core index. The lateral problem uses TM because the main E-field of the TE-like mode is perpendicular to the sidewalls.
  • The slab solver is a transfer matrix on (ψ, p·ψ’), p = 1 for TE and 1/n² for TM; guided modes are the zeros of the mismatch at the right cladding.
  • Ask students to run neff_strip for w = 0.3 µm: the mode approaches cut-off and n_g changes rapidly: this is why single-mode widths below ~400 nm are sensitive to fabrication.
  • The middle panel is the important lesson for model extraction: the 6th-order polynomial passes through every data point and still has large errors 30 nm outside the range.

Straight waveguide: temperature-dependent fitted model

\[ n_{\text{Eff}}^{\text{Str}}(\lambda, T) = N_0(T) + N_1(T)\left(\frac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\sigma_\lambda}\right) + N_2(T)\left(\frac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\sigma_\lambda}\right)^2 \]

\[ \begin{cases} N_0(T) = n_0 + n_1\left(\frac{T - T_0}{\sigma_T}\right) + n_2\left(\frac{T - T_0}{\sigma_T}\right)^2 \\ N_1(T) = n_3 + n_4\left(\frac{T - T_0}{\sigma_T}\right) + n_5\left(\frac{T - T_0}{\sigma_T}\right)^2 \\ N_2(T) = n_6 + n_7\left(\frac{T - T_0}{\sigma_T}\right) + n_8\left(\frac{T - T_0}{\sigma_T}\right)^2 \end{cases} \]

from: “Temperature Effects on Silicon-on-Insulator (SOI) Racetrack Resonators: A Coupled Analytic and 2-D Finite Difference Approach”

  • Nine coefficients n_0…n_8 describe the waveguide over wavelength and temperature. The normalisation by σ_λ and σ_T keeps all coefficients of order one, which avoids ill-conditioned fits.
  • The temperature dependence is dominated by dn/dT of silicon (1.86 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹); resonances shift by about 80 pm/K, which is why every ring in a real system needs a heater and a control loop.
  • The same structure (polynomial in normalised variables, coefficients themselves polynomials) is used for width and thickness in PDKs that support corner models.

Results

  • Compare 1550 and 1310 nm: at 1310 the mode is more confined, so n_eff is higher and less sensitive to width.
  • Point out the curvature: n_eff saturates at large width (the mode is fully in the core) and drops quickly near cut-off. The derivative dn_eff/dw is the sensitivity that drives the yield discussion in Section F.

Directional Coupler Model

Coupling coefficient:

\[\kappa = \kappa_0 \exp\left(-\frac{g}{g_0}\right), \qquad \kappa = \frac{\pi\,(n_{even}-n_{odd})}{\lambda}\]

where:

  • g = gap between waveguides
  • \(g_0\) = characteristic decay length (evanescent tail)

Transfer matrix:

\[[T] = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\kappa L) & -j\sin(\kappa L) \\ -j\sin(\kappa L) & \cos(\kappa L) \end{bmatrix}\]

Power splitting ratio:

\[\eta = \sin^2(\kappa L + \phi_{bend})\]

(\(\phi_{bend}\): extra coupling in the input/output bends)

Wavelength dependence:

\[\kappa(\lambda) = \kappa_0 \left(1 + \alpha_\lambda(\lambda - \lambda_0)\right)\]

  • Two equivalent pictures: coupled-mode theory (κ, energy exchange) and supermodes (even and odd modes beating). κ = πΔn/λ links them and is what we compute in Python.
  • The exponential dependence on the gap is the dominant sensitivity: a 10 nm change of gap can change κ by ~10%.
  • The bend contribution φ_bend is often 10–30% of the total coupling for short couplers and must be in the model; otherwise the extracted κ_0 is wrong.

Python: coupler from supermodes

def dc_kappa(gap, w=0.5, h=0.22, lam=1.55, nsi=3.476, nox=1.444):
    n_slab = slab_modes([nox, nsi, nox], [h], lam, "TE")[0]
    even, odd = slab_modes([nox, n_slab, nox, n_slab, nox], [w, gap, w], lam, "TM")[:2]
    return np.pi*(even - odd)/lam                 # 1/um ;  L_c = pi/(2 kappa)

def coupler_S(kappa_L):                           # 4-port: in1, in2, out1, out2
    t, k = np.cos(kappa_L), np.sin(kappa_L)
    return np.array([[0, 0, t, 1j*k], [0, 0, 1j*k, t], [t, 1j*k, 0, 0], [1j*k, t, 0, 0]])
  • Left: L_c grows exponentially with the gap (straight line on a log scale) and is longer at shorter wavelength because the mode is more confined.
  • Right: the 50:50 coupler designed at 1550 nm goes from 36% to 66% over 100 nm. This is the reason for broadband alternatives: MMIs, adiabatic couplers, or bent/asymmetric couplers.
  • The 100% coupler is flat to first order at the design point (maximum of sin²), the 50:50 is at the steepest point: a 3 dB coupler is the most sensitive design.

Ring Resonator Compact Model

All-Pass and Add-Drop Formulation

All-pass (self-coupling \(t\), round-trip amplitude \(a\), phase \(\phi = \beta L\)):

\[T_{thr} = \frac{a^2 - 2at\cos\phi + t^2}{1 - 2at\cos\phi + (at)^2}\]

Add-drop (drop port):

\[T_{drop} = \frac{(1-t_1^2)(1-t_2^2)\,a}{1 - 2at_1t_2\cos\phi + (at_1t_2)^2}\]

Key parameters:

\[FSR = \frac{\lambda^2}{n_g L}, \qquad Q_L = \frac{\pi n_g L \sqrt{ta}}{\lambda (1 - ta)}\]

\[\text{Finesse} = \frac{FSR}{\Delta\lambda_{FWHM}}, \quad \Delta\lambda_{FWHM} = \frac{\lambda}{Q_L}\]

Coupling regimes: under (\(t > a\)), critical (\(t = a\), zero through-port), over (\(t < a\)).

Model inputs:

  • Radius R (geometry) → L = 2πR (+ straight sections)
  • Coupling coefficient κ (from the coupler model)
  • Propagation loss α → \(a = 10^{-\alpha_{dB} L/20}\)
  • Effective/group index (from the waveguide model)
  • Stress that the ring model is not a new model: it is the coupler model plus the waveguide model in a loop. That is exactly how the netlist solver will build it.
  • Critical coupling (t = a) gives zero transmission at resonance: used for modulators and notch filters. It requires knowing the loss, which is why test structures with several gaps are always on the mask.
  • Q_L is the loaded Q; intrinsic Q is obtained with t = 1. Example: R = 10 µm, 2 dB/cm, κ² = 5% gives Q_L ≈ 2 × 10⁴.

Python: ring amplitude, phase and group delay

def ring_allpass(phi, a, t):
    """All-pass ring field transmission."""
    return (t - a*np.exp(-1j*phi)) / \
           (1 - a*t*np.exp(-1j*phi))

phi = np.linspace(-0.3, 0.3, 2001)
for t in (0.995, 0.98, 0.90):        # a = 0.98
    H = ring_allpass(phi, 0.98, t)
    T_dB  = 20*np.log10(abs(H))
    phase = np.unwrap(np.angle(H))
    delay = -np.gradient(phase, phi)  # in round trips
  • Under-coupled: shallow dip, negative delay (“fast light”).
  • Over-coupled: shallow dip, full 2π phase, large positive delay: used for optical delay lines and dispersion compensation.
  • The amplitude of under- and over-coupled rings can look identical; the phase tells them apart. Measuring only |T|² you cannot know if you are under or over-coupled: you need either a phase measurement or several devices with different gaps.
  • Group delay in units of round-trip time: multiply by L n_g / c. For R = 10 µm, one round trip is ≈ 0.9 ps; 20 round trips = 18 ps of delay, the basis of ring-based true-time delays in Section C.

Mach-Zehnder Interferometer Model

Interference-Based Modulation

Transfer function (bar port, 50:50 couplers):

\[T = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \cos\left(\Delta\phi\right)\right], \qquad \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi\, n_{eff} \Delta L}{\lambda} + \Delta\phi_{PS}\]

Phase shift modeling:

\[\Delta \phi_{PS} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left[\Delta n_{eff}(V) \cdot L\right]\]

For electro-optic modulation:

\[\Delta n_{eff}(V) = \Gamma \cdot \frac{\Delta n}{\Delta V} \cdot V\]

where \(\Gamma\) = mode overlap factor

Extinction ratio:

\[ER = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{T_{max}}{T_{min}}\right)\]

limited by the coupler imbalance: \(ER \approx 20\log_{10}\!\frac{1}{|1-2\eta|}\) for a coupler with ratio \(\eta\).

Free spectral range:

\[FSR_{MZI} = \frac{\lambda^2}{n_g \Delta L}\]

  • Which port is “bar” depends on the coupler convention (see the extra slide on reciprocity): with jκ couplers, a balanced MZI is fully in the cross state.
  • ER limit: a 48:52 coupler (η = 0.48) gives ER ≈ 28 dB; 45:55 gives 20 dB. This is why high-ER MZIs use MMIs or tunable couplers.
  • n_eff sets the position of the fringes, n_g sets their spacing: same lesson as for rings.

Python: connecting models — a netlist solver

All component S-matrices are stacked block-diagonally (\(S\)); a connection matrix \(C\) pairs internal ports (\(a_i = C\,b_i\)). Eliminating internal waves:

\[S_{ext} = S_{ee} + S_{ei}\,(I - C S_{ii})^{-1} C\, S_{ie}\]

def solve_circuit(insts, nets, wl):
    """insts: {name: (S[nw,p,p], port_names)}
       nets : [("A.p", "B.q"), ...]"""
    idx, names, off = {}, [], 0
    for n, (S, ports) in insts.items():
        for j, pn in enumerate(ports):
            idx[f"{n}.{pn}"] = off + j; names.append(f"{n}.{pn}")
        off += len(ports)
    S = np.zeros((len(wl), off, off), complex); o = 0
    for Sb, ports in insts.values():
        p = len(ports); S[:, o:o+p, o:o+p] = Sb; o += p
    C = np.zeros((off, off))
    for a, b in nets:
        C[idx[a], idx[b]] = C[idx[b], idx[a]] = 1
    i = np.where(C.sum(1) > 0)[0]; e = np.where(C.sum(1) == 0)[0]
    Sii, Sie = S[:, i][:, :, i], S[:, i][:, :, e]
    Sei, See = S[:, e][:, :, i], S[:, e][:, :, e]
    Cii = C[np.ix_(i, i)]
    X = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(len(i)) - Cii @ Sii, Cii @ Sie)
    return See + Sei @ X, [names[k] for k in e]

An unbalanced MZI in four lines:

insts = {"dc1": coupler(np.sqrt(.5), nw), "dc2": coupler(np.sqrt(.5), nw),
         "arm1": waveguide(100, wl, neff), "arm2": waveguide(150, wl, neff)}
nets = [("dc1.out1", "arm1.a"), ("arm1.b", "dc2.in1"),
        ("dc1.out2", "arm2.a"), ("arm2.b", "dc2.in2")]
S, ports = solve_circuit(insts, nets, wl)

This ~25-line function is the core of IPKISS/Caphe, Lumerical Interconnect, SAX, Simphony.

  • Walk through the algebra: b = S a for all ports; for internal ports a_i = C b_i (what leaves one port enters the connected one). Solve for a_i and substitute: one linear solve per wavelength.
  • The method handles feedback automatically (rings, Fabry–Pérot, reflections), unlike the T-matrix cascade.
  • The FSR from the plot, 12 nm, matches λ²/(n_g ΔL) with n_g = 4.02 and ΔL = 50 µm. Ask students what changes if they use n_eff instead of n_g in the FSR formula (a 60% error).
  • Exercise: add a phase shifter instance in arm 1 and sweep its phase to turn the MZI into a switch.

Higher-order filters from the same solver

Series-coupled rings (N rings, N + 1 couplers):

def coupled_rings(Kpow, wl, R=5.0):
    insts, nets = {}, []
    for i, K in enumerate(Kpow):
        insts[f"c{i}"] = coupler(np.sqrt(K), len(wl))
    for i in range(1, len(Kpow)):
        insts[f"r{i}a"] = waveguide(np.pi*R, wl, neff)
        insts[f"r{i}b"] = waveguide(np.pi*R, wl, neff)
        nets += [(f"c{i-1}.out2", f"r{i}a.a"),
                 (f"r{i}a.b",     f"c{i}.in1"),
                 (f"c{i}.out1",   f"r{i}b.a"),
                 (f"r{i}b.b",     f"c{i-1}.in2")]
    return solve_circuit(insts, nets, wl)

Maximally flat (Butterworth) couplings: \(K_2 = K_1^2/4\) (2nd order), \(K_2 = K_3 = K_1^2/8\) (3rd order).

  • Higher order: flat passband, roll-off of 20·N dB/decade, better adjacent-channel rejection.
  • Demux: a radius change of only ≈ 8 nm per channel moves the resonance by 1.6 nm. The same sensitivity is the yield problem of Section F.
  • Box-like responses matter for WDM: a flat top tolerates laser drift and filter misalignment; steep skirts reduce crosstalk.
  • The coupling recipe (K2 = K1²/4) comes from Little et al., J. Lightwave Technol. 15, 998 (1997). In practice all rings must also be resonant at the same wavelength: a 3rd-order filter needs three heaters and a calibration algorithm.
  • The demux crosstalk (−20 dB level between channels) is set by the Lorentzian tails of first-order rings; replacing each ring by a 2nd-order filter improves it dramatically.

Grating Coupler Model

Vertical Coupling Efficiency

Coupling efficiency (Gaussian approximation):

\[\eta(\lambda) = \eta_0 \exp\left(-\frac{(\lambda - \lambda_c)^2}{2\sigma_\lambda^2}\right)\]

Angular dependence:

\[\eta(\theta) = \eta_0 \exp\left(-\frac{(\theta - \theta_0)^2}{2\sigma_\theta^2}\right)\]

Centre wavelength vs angle (phase matching):

\[\lambda_c(\theta) = \Lambda\,(n_{eff} - n_{c}\sin\theta) \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{d\lambda_c}{d\theta} = -\Lambda\, n_c \cos\theta\]

Model parameters:

  • Peak coupling efficiency: \(\eta_0\)
  • Center wavelength: \(\lambda_c\)
  • Bandwidth: \(\sigma_\lambda\) (1 dB BW ≈ 30–40 nm)
  • Angular tolerance: \(\sigma_\theta\)
  • Back-reflection \(S_{11}\), polarization dependence
  • Link to Module 3: the Bragg condition defines λ_c; a 1° change of fibre angle shifts λ_c by roughly 10–15 nm for a 630 nm period, which is why fibre angle is a model parameter.
  • The back-reflection S11 of a grating coupler (−15 to −20 dB) creates Fabry–Pérot fringes between two couplers; it must be in the model or the simulated spectrum will look cleaner than the measured one.

Phase Shifter Models

Thermo-Optic and Electro-Optic

Thermo-optic:

\[\Delta \phi_{TO} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \frac{dn}{dT} \Delta T \cdot L\]

Temperature vs power:

\[\Delta T = R_{th} \cdot P_{heater}\]

Thermal RC time constant:

\[\tau_{th} = R_{th} \cdot C_{th}\]

Typical values:

  • dn/dT (Si) = 1.86×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹
  • \(P_\pi\) ≈ 20–25 mW (no undercut), < 5 mW (undercut)
  • τ_th ≈ 1-10 μs

Carrier depletion:

\[\Delta \phi_{CD} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \Gamma \Delta n(V) \cdot L\]

Carrier-induced index change (Soref, 1550 nm):

\[\Delta n = -8.8 \times 10^{-22} \Delta N_e - 8.5 \times 10^{-18} (\Delta N_h)^{0.8}\]

Junction capacitance:

\[C_j(V) = \frac{C_0}{\sqrt{1 + V/V_{bi}}}\]

Typical values:

  • V_π L ≈ 2-4 V·cm
  • C ≈ 10-50 fF
  • f_3dB > 40 GHz
  • The P_π of a heater is independent of its length to first order: a longer heater needs a smaller ΔT but heats a larger volume. We compute P_π ≈ 22 mW with a 2D thermal model in Section E.
  • The plasma-dispersion model is the one derived in Module 4; in a compact model it is often replaced by a fitted Δn_eff(V) and α(V) table from TCAD.
  • C_j(V) matters for the driver: a smaller capacitance at higher reverse bias increases bandwidth but V_π also increases.

Photodetector Compact Model

Responsivity:

\[R(\lambda) = \frac{q\lambda}{hc}\eta(\lambda)\]

where:

  • η(λ) = quantum efficiency
  • q = elementary charge
  • h = Planck’s constant

Photocurrent:

\[I_{ph} = R(\lambda) \cdot P_{opt} + I_{dark}\]

Frequency response:

\[H(f) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_{3dB})^2}}\]

Bandwidth limitations:

\[\frac{1}{f_{3dB}^2} = \frac{1}{f_{RC}^2} + \frac{1}{f_{transit}^2}, \quad f_{RC} = \frac{1}{2\pi R C},\; f_{tr} \approx \frac{0.45\, v_{sat}}{d}\]

Noise model (one-sided, bandwidth B):

  • Shot noise: \(i_{shot}^2 = 2qI_{ph}B\)
  • Dark current: \(i_{dark}^2 = 2qI_{dark}B\)
  • Thermal noise: \(i_{th}^2 = 4kTB/R\)
  • Laser RIN at the detector: \(i_{RIN}^2 = RIN \cdot I_{ph}^2 B\)
  • At 1550 nm, R = 1.25 A/W × η; a Ge detector with η = 80% gives 1 A/W.
  • The four noise terms are the ones used in the analog-link noise figure in Section C: thermal dominates at low photocurrent, shot noise in the middle, RIN at high photocurrent (it grows as I²).
  • Transit-time and RC limits trade against each other through the absorber thickness d (Module 5).

Model Parameter Extraction

From Simulation to Compact Model

Extraction workflow:

  1. Design of Experiments (DOE)
    • Sweep critical parameters
    • Cover operating range
    • Include fabrication tolerances
  2. Physical simulation
    • FDTD/FEM for each DOE point
    • Extract S-parameters
    • Calculate derived quantities
  1. Fitting procedure
    • Choose model form (analytical, polynomial, etc.)
    • Least-squares fitting
    • Validate fit quality (R² > 0.99)
  2. Verification
    • Test against independent data
    • Check edge cases
    • Validate causality
  • DOE: the cost is driven by the number of dimensions. 5 parameters × 5 levels = 3125 FDTD runs; a Latin-hypercube or sparse-grid DOE reduces this to a few hundred.
  • Always keep some simulation points out of the fit (verification set). R² > 0.99 on the training set says nothing about interpolation quality.
  • After fabrication, the same workflow is repeated with measured data: the PDK model is re-centred on silicon.

Flowchart of model parameter extraction

  • Follow the loop: simulation data → fit → validation; if validation fails, either the model form or the DOE is wrong.
  • The loop closes with measurement: test structures on every MPW run are used to update the model parameters.

Fitting Techniques: Mathematical Approaches

Polynomial fitting: \(y(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots + a_n x^n\)

Advantages:

  • Simple to implement
  • Fast evaluation
  • Smooth derivatives

Limitations:

  • Can oscillate between points
  • Extrapolation unreliable
  • High-order can overfit

Multi-dimensional fitting: \(f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{i,j,\ldots} c_{ij\ldots} \phi_i(x_1)\phi_j(x_2)\cdots\)

c = np.polyfit((lam - 1.55)/0.05, neff_data, 2)   # normalise x!
model = lambda lam: np.polyval(c, (lam - 1.55)/0.05)
from scipy.interpolate import CubicSpline, RegularGridInterpolator
spline = CubicSpline(w_data, neff_data)
lut2d  = RegularGridInterpolator((W, H), neff_table)  # n_eff(w, h)

Spline interpolation: \(y(x) = S_i(x) \text{ for } x_{i-1} \leq x < x_i\)

Cubic splines:

  • Continuous second derivative
  • No oscillations
  • Local control

B-splines:

  • Better for multi-dimensional
  • Efficient evaluation
  • Flexible control
  • Normalising the variable (λ − λ0)/σ before fitting is not cosmetic: with raw λ in metres the Vandermonde matrix is singular to machine precision.
  • Good practice: choose the lowest polynomial order whose residual is below the fabrication uncertainty (here 10⁻³ in n_eff ≈ 0.6 nm resonance shift). Anything more is fitting noise.
  • The 2D RegularGridInterpolator is the model used in the yield Monte Carlo of Section F.

Extra: causality, passivity and vector fitting

Optional deep-dive.

Why care? A tabulated \(S(\omega)\) must be converted to an impulse response for time-domain simulation. If the table is not causal, the impulse response starts before \(t = 0\).

Kramers–Kronig: for a causal response, amplitude and phase are not independent:

\[\theta(\omega) = -\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathcal{P}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\ln|H(\omega')|}{\omega - \omega'}\, d\omega'\]

(minimum-phase systems). Fitting \(|S|\) and \(\arg S\) separately easily violates it.

Passivity: eigenvalues of \(S^\dagger S \le 1\) at every frequency.

Vector fitting (Gustavsen & Semlyen, 1999): approximate the data by a sum of poles

\[S(s) \approx \sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{r_k}{s - p_k} + d, \qquad \text{Re}(p_k) < 0\]

  • Stable poles ⇒ causal by construction
  • Each pole pair = one damped resonance (rings!) or a delay approximant
  • Time domain: each term is a first-order ODE, solved recursively ⇒ fast transient simulation
  • Passivity enforced afterwards by small perturbations of \(r_k\)

Used inside Interconnect, IPKISS (Caphe), ADS, and SPICE-based photonic co-simulation.

  • Photonic peculiarity: optical S-parameters are band-pass around 193 THz and include long delays (a 1 mm waveguide = 14 ps), which require many poles. Tools first remove the linear phase (delay) and fit the baseband envelope.
  • Connection to the ring model: a ring is exactly a pole near the unit circle in the z-domain; its Lorentzian is the pole term above.

  • Line-edge roughness
  • Thickness variation
  • Material properties
  • Temperature gradients

Statistical model:

\[p_{actual} = p_{nominal} + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_p)\]

  • A complete model has hierarchical variation: wafer-to-wafer, die-to-die (across wafer), within-die (local mismatch). Two rings 100 µm apart see almost the same thickness (correlated) but different line-edge roughness (uncorrelated).
  • Correlation matters: a ring and its neighbour shift together, so a WDM filter keeps its channel spacing even if the absolute wavelength moves. Section F uses this.

Monte Carlo simulation:

  1. Sample parameters from distributions
  2. Run circuit simulation
  3. Collect statistics
  4. Calculate yield and performance spread

Typical variations:

  • Waveguide width: \(\sigma = 5-10\) nm
  • Layer thickness: \(\sigma = 2-5\) nm
  • Coupling coefficient: \(\sigma = 5-10\%\)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

  • Number of samples: the statistical error on a yield Y estimated from N samples is sqrt(Y(1−Y)/N); 10⁴ samples give ±0.5% at 50% yield.

RF and Optical Signal Processing

Microwave photonics (MWP) combines:

  • Optical signal processing
  • RF/microwave engineering
  • Enables ultra-wide bandwidth
  • Low loss signal distribution

Key applications:

  • Antenna remoting
  • Radar signal processing
  • 5G/6G fronthaul
  • Software-defined radio
  • Arbitrary waveform generation
  • RF spectrum analysis

Advantages:

  • THz bandwidth
  • Low loss distribution (0.2 dB/km in fibre vs ~1 dB/m for coax at 10 GHz)
  • Immunity to EMI
  • Compact and lightweight
  • Reconfigurable

Detection:

\[i_{RF}(t) = R \cdot |E_{opt}(t)|^2\]

Overall transfer function:

\[H_{EOE}(\omega) = H_{mod}(\omega) \cdot H_{opt}(\omega) \cdot H_{det}(\omega)\]

  • Phase modulation alone gives no RF signal after square-law detection (|E|² is constant): the optical processing (filter, dispersion, interferometer) must convert phase into intensity. This is the principle of many MWP filters.

Analog Optical Links

Performance Metrics (MZM biased at quadrature)

Link gain (matched modulator input, detector into \(R_L\)):

\[G_{link} = \frac{P_{RF,out}}{P_{RF,in}} = \left(\frac{\pi I_{dc}}{V_\pi}\right)^2 R_S R_L\]

  • \(I_{dc} = \mathcal{R}\,P_{opt,avg}\) = DC photocurrent
  • \(V_\pi\) = half-wave voltage of the modulator
  • \(R_S\), \(R_L\) = source and load impedances

Gain grows as \(I_{dc}^2\): more optical power or a lower \(V_\pi\) is the only way to a lossless link.

Output noise (W/Hz):

\[N_{out} = G k T_0 + k T_0 + 2qI_{dc}R_L + RIN\cdot I_{dc}^2 R_L\]

Noise figure:

\[NF = 10\log_{10}\frac{N_{out}}{G\,kT_0}\]

Spurious-free dynamic range (3rd order):

\[SFDR = \frac{2}{3}\left(OIP_3 - N_{out}\right)\Big|_{dB}, \quad OIP_3 = 4I_{dc}^2R_L\]

Typical performance: Gain −20 to +10 dB · NF 15–40 dB · SFDR 100–120 dB·Hz2/3

  • Derivation of G: at quadrature P = P_avg[1 + sin(πV/V_π)], so i = I_dc·πv/V_π for small v. Output power i²R_L/2, input power v²/(2R_S): ratio (πI_dc/V_π)²R_S R_L.
  • Example: I_dc = 10 mA, V_π = 3 V, 50 Ω: G = −5.6 dB. With V_π = 1 V: +3.9 dB.
  • The four noise terms: amplified input thermal noise, thermal noise of the load, shot noise, laser RIN. The last two grow with I_dc (as I and I²), so NF stops improving at high power: RIN sets the floor.
  • With a resistively matched detector, the signal terms are divided by 4 (half the current flows in the matching resistor); textbooks (Cox, Analog Optical Links) give both cases.

Gain, noise figure and SFDR vs photocurrent

kB, q, T0 = 1.380649e-23, 1.602e-19, 290
def link_metrics(Idc, Vpi=3, RS=50, RL=50,
                 rin_db=-155):
    G = (np.pi*Idc/Vpi)**2 * RS*RL
    rin = 10**(rin_db/10)
    Nout = (G*kB*T0 + kB*T0
            + 2*q*Idc*RL + rin*Idc**2*RL)
    NF = 10*np.log10(Nout/(G*kB*T0))
    OIP3 = 4*Idc**2*RL
    SFDR = 2/3*10*np.log10(OIP3/Nout)
    return 10*np.log10(G), NF, SFDR

print(link_metrics(10e-3))
# → (-5.6 dB, 32.0 dB, 107 dB·Hz^2/3)
  • Low \(I_{dc}\): thermal noise dominates, NF falls as \(1/I_{dc}^2\).
  • High \(I_{dc}\): RIN noise grows like the signal ⇒ NF and SFDR saturate. A −165 dB/Hz laser is worth 10 dB of NF.
  • Integrated links lose optical power in couplers ⇒ low \(I_{dc}\) ⇒ high NF. Low-loss I/O (Module 3) is an RF specification.
  • Read the NF curve: below 1 mA the link is a 40–50 dB NF device, useless without RF pre-amplification. Above 10 mA, with a good laser, NF approaches 20 dB.
  • The photodetector must handle the current: 50–100 mA requires high-power (e.g. modified uni-travelling-carrier) photodiodes.
  • Discussion: why does SFDR saturate? Both OIP3 and N_out (RIN term) scale as I²: their ratio becomes constant.

Two-tone test: where SFDR comes from

Two tones \(V\sin\Omega_1 t + V\sin\Omega_2 t\) into the MZM at quadrature. With \(x = \pi V/V_\pi\) (Jacobi–Anger):

\[i(\Omega_1) = 2I_{dc}\,J_1(x)J_0(x) \approx I_{dc}\,x\] \[i(2\Omega_1-\Omega_2) = 2I_{dc}\,J_2(x)J_1(x) \approx \frac{I_{dc}\,x^3}{8}\]

  • fundamental: slope 1 dB/dB
  • IM3: slope 3 dB/dB
  • intercept at \(x^2 = 8\) ⇒ \(OIP_3 = 4I_{dc}^2R_L\)

SFDR = input range between the noise floor and the point where IM3 reaches the noise floor.

from scipy.special import jv
x  = np.pi*Vamp/Vpi
i1 = 2*Idc*jv(1, x)*jv(0, x)     # fundamental
i3 = 2*Idc*jv(2, x)*jv(1, x)     # IM3
  • IM3 products at 2Ω1 − Ω2 and 2Ω2 − Ω1 fall inside the band and cannot be filtered: this is why third-order distortion defines the dynamic range of sub-octave systems.
  • The intercept is never reached: the real curves compress before (the sine saturates) and show nulls of the Bessel functions. IP3 is an extrapolation used as a figure of merit.
  • SFDR in dB·Hz^(2/3): for a bandwidth B, subtract (2/3)·10log10(B). For B = 100 MHz, a 107 dB·Hz^(2/3) link has 107 − 53 = 54 dB of dynamic range.
  • Linearisation techniques: dual-parallel MZM, ring-assisted MZI (RAMZI), predistortion.

Extra: Jacobi–Anger and the MZM harmonics

Optional deep-dive.

At quadrature, \(P(t) = \dfrac{P_0}{2}\left[1 + \sin\!\big(x\sin\Omega t\big)\right]\). Using

\[\sin(x\sin\Omega t) = 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} J_{2k+1}(x)\,\sin\big((2k+1)\Omega t\big)\]

  • Only odd harmonics: quadrature bias cancels all even-order distortion (2nd harmonic, IM2).
  • Fundamental amplitude \(\propto 2J_1(x)\), 3rd harmonic \(\propto 2J_3(x) \approx x^3/24\).
  • Moving the bias away from quadrature (e.g. low-biasing to reduce RIN and shot noise) re-introduces even orders: acceptable only in sub-octave systems.

Two tones: \(\sin(x\sin\Omega_1 t + x\sin\Omega_2 t)\) expands into products \(J_m(x)J_n(x)\) at \(m\Omega_1 + n\Omega_2\); the in-band IM3 term is \((m, n) = (2, -1)\), which gives the \(J_2J_1\) term of the previous slide.

  • Do the expansion on the board using e^{jx sinθ} = Σ J_n(x) e^{jnθ}.
  • Low-biasing: biasing towards the null reduces I_dc (less shot and RIN noise) while the signal slope decreases less; it improves NF at the price of second-order distortion.

Intensity Modulation

Direct and External Modulation

Direct modulation:

  • Modulate laser drive current
  • Simple implementation
  • Limited bandwidth (~10–30 GHz)
  • Chirp issues

Laser rate equations:

\(\frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{I(t)}{qV} - \frac{N}{\tau_n} - v_g G(N) S\)

\(\frac{dS}{dt} = \Gamma v_g G(N) S + \beta \frac{N}{\tau_n} - \frac{S}{\tau_p}\)

Small-signal response: \(H(f) = \dfrac{f_r^2}{f_r^2 - f^2 + j f\gamma/2\pi}\), with \(f_r \propto \sqrt{I - I_{th}}\)

External modulation:

  • Separate laser and modulator
  • Higher bandwidth (>50 GHz)
  • Better linearity
  • Chirp control possible

Types:

  • Mach-Zehnder modulator (MZM)
  • Electro-absorption modulator (EAM)
  • Ring resonator modulator

Modulation index:

\(m(\omega) = \dfrac{\pi V_{RF}}{V_\pi}\, H_{TW}(\omega)\), \(H_{TW}\) = travelling-wave electrode response

  • Direct modulation couples intensity and frequency (chirp, linewidth enhancement factor α_H); after fibre dispersion this converts to intensity distortion.
  • The relaxation-oscillation frequency increases with the square root of the bias above threshold, so DMLs for high speed run at high current, which increases RIN near f_r.
  • For external modulators the bandwidth is set by the electrode: RF loss and velocity mismatch (next slide).

Direct vs external modulation: frequency response

H_dml = fr**2/(fr**2 - f**2 + 1j*f*gamma)   # gamma = K fr^2 + gamma0
g = (alpha_rf(f) + 1j*2*np.pi*f*(n_rf - n_g)/c) * L
H_tw = (1 - np.exp(-g))/g                    # walk-off + loss
  • DML: the peak gives a flat-ish response up to about 1.5 f_r, then a 40 dB/decade roll-off. Damping (K-factor) limits the maximum bandwidth to ≈ 2π√2/K.
  • MZM: with silicon PN electrodes the RF loss (≈1 dB/(cm·√GHz)) is the main limit; index mismatch matters for long electrodes. Shorter electrodes give more bandwidth but a higher V_π: the V_π·L vs bandwidth trade-off.

Optical Filtering

RF Signal Processing in Optical Domain

Advantages of optical filtering:

  • Ultra-wide bandwidth and tuning range
  • Flat passband
  • Sharp roll-off
  • Reconfigurable
  • Low insertion loss

Ring resonator filter (drop port, field):

\(H(\omega) = \dfrac{-\kappa_1\kappa_2\sqrt{a}\,e^{-j\phi/2}}{1 - a t_1 t_2 e^{-j\phi}}, \quad \phi = \frac{\omega n_{eff} L}{c}\)

Cascaded / coupled rings:

  • Higher-order response
  • Sharper transitions
  • Box-like passband

Applications:

  • RF channel selection
  • Interference rejection
  • Sideband filtering (SSB)
  • Carrier suppression

Resolution: a ring with Q = 10⁶ has a 190 MHz linewidth: an RF filter selecting a 200 MHz channel at any frequency from 1 to 40 GHz, just by tuning the heater.

  • MWP notch filter principle: modulate with single sideband, place a ring resonance on the sideband: the RF signal at that frequency disappears. Rejection > 50 dB and GHz tuning are achieved on chip.
  • The limit of optical RF filters is resolution: an RF filter of 10 MHz requires optical Q ≈ 2 × 10⁷, beyond silicon (but reachable in SiN or with Brillouin scattering).

True Time Delay

Optical Beamforming

Concept:

  • Delay optical signals for phased array
  • Wide instantaneous bandwidth
  • No beam squint

Implementation approaches:

1. Optical fiber / waveguide delays:

\(\tau = \frac{n_g L}{c}\)

  • Fixed delays
  • Low loss
  • Large footprint

2. Switchable delays:

  • Binary-weighted stages
  • 2^N delay states
  • Thermo-optic switches

3. Dispersive delay:

  • Chromatic dispersion
  • Wavelength-dependent
  • Compact implementation

4. Cavity-based:

  • Ring resonators (over-coupled, Section A)
  • Slow light structures
  • Delay × bandwidth product limited

Delay requirement:

\(\tau_{max} = \frac{D \sin(\theta_{max})}{c}\)

where D = array aperture

  • Example: D = 24 cm (16 elements at λ/2 for 10 GHz), θ = 30°: τ_max = 0.4 ns, i.e. 2.9 cm of Si waveguide (n_g = 4.2) or 8 cm of fibre. On chip, long spirals with < 0.1 dB/cm loss (SiN) are used.
  • Ring delays: delay × bandwidth ≈ constant (≈ 1 per ring), so broadband delays need many rings in cascade.

Phase shift vs true-time delay: beam squint

Element \(n\) at position \(nd\), steering to \(\theta_0\):

  • phase shifter: \(\varphi_n = n k_c d\sin\theta_0\) (fixed phase, set at \(f_c\))
  • TTD: \(\tau_n = n d\sin\theta_0/c\) ⇒ phase \(2\pi f\tau_n\) scales with \(f\)
k  = 2*np.pi*f/c
ps = np.exp(1j*n*(k*d*np.sin(th)
            - kc*d*np.sin(th0))).sum(0)
tt = np.exp(1j*n*k*d*(np.sin(th)
            - np.sin(th0))).sum(0)

Phase-shifter beam direction: \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{f_c}{f}\sin\theta_0\)

  • At 6 GHz the phase-shifter array points at 56° instead of 30°: for a wideband radar pulse, different frequencies go in different directions (squint).
  • TTD keeps the beam fixed; only the beam width changes (wider at low frequency because the aperture is smaller in wavelengths).
  • This is the strongest argument for photonic beamforming: true delays over a multi-octave bandwidth.

Optical Frequency Combs

Multi-Wavelength Sources

Generation methods:

  1. Mode-locked lasers
    • Femtosecond pulses
    • Broad spectrum
    • Phase coherence
  2. Electro-optic (EO) combs
    • Cascaded modulators
    • Controllable spacing
    • Flat amplitude
  3. Microresonator combs (Kerr combs)
    • Nonlinear generation
    • On-chip integration
    • Four-wave mixing

Comb equation:

\(f_n = f_0 + n \cdot f_{rep}\)

where:

  • f_0 = offset frequency
  • f_rep = repetition rate
  • n = mode number

Applications:

  • WDM sources
  • Optical arbitrary waveform generation
  • Frequency metrology
  • Spectroscopy
  • Low-noise microwave generation (beat of comb lines)
  • EO comb: a phase modulator driven at f_m produces lines at f_c + n f_m with amplitudes J_n(β); spacing is set by the RF source, so it is exactly controllable.
  • Kerr microcomb: a high-Q SiN ring pumped by a CW laser; four-wave mixing generates lines at the FSR spacing; in the soliton state the envelope is sech² and the lines are phase-locked.
  • MWP use: beating two comb lines on a photodiode generates a microwave with the comb’s low phase noise.

Combs: spectra

from scipy.special import jv
n = np.arange(-12, 13)
P_n = jv(n, beta)**2          # EO comb line powers

Number of lines ≈ \(2\beta + 1\) (Carson’s rule): β = 5 rad needs \(V \approx 1.6\,V_\pi\).

  • EO comb: non-flat (Bessel nulls); flattening by cascading an intensity modulator (time gating) or a second phase modulator.
  • Microcomb: 100+ lines from a single ring and one pump laser; spacing 10 GHz–1 THz set by the ring FSR.
  • Carson’s rule: the number of significant sidebands is about 2(β + 1). Driving a phase modulator at β = 5 requires about 1.6 V_π, which is why EO combs use resonant electrodes or LiNbO₃ modulators.
  • Microcomb lines can be used as many parallel WDM carriers from one laser: a key topic for data-centre transceivers.

RF Photonic Signal Processing

Advanced Processing Techniques

Photonic mixing:

  • Two RF signals on different wavelengths
  • Combine in photodetector
  • Generates mixing products

\(i_{PD} \propto |E_1 + E_2|^2 = |E_1|^2 + |E_2|^2 + 2|E_1||E_2|\cos(\Delta\omega t + \Delta\varphi)\)

Applications:

  • Frequency conversion
  • Phase detection
  • I/Q demodulation
  • mm-wave / THz generation (heterodyne)

Photonic differentiation:

  • Ring at critical coupling, or MZI at a null
  • Transfer function \(\propto j(\omega-\omega_0)\) near resonance
  • Ultra-wideband operation

Photonic integration:

  • Accumulates signal over time
  • Implemented with a high-Q (lossless) ring or dispersion
  • Signal enhancement

Hilbert transform:

  • 90° phase shift
  • SSB generation
  • Implemented with MZI / phase-shifted gratings
  • The beat term carries the phase difference Δφ: if the two lasers are independent, their phase noises add; phase locking or deriving both lines from one comb gives a clean microwave.
  • Near a zero of the transfer function, any linear filter behaves like a differentiator; a critically coupled ring has its zero exactly on resonance.

Heterodyne generation and photonic differentiation

H = tc*(1 - np.exp(-1j*phi)) / (1 - tc**2*np.exp(-1j*phi))   # critical ring, phi = 2*pi*f/FSR
E_out = np.fft.ifft(np.fft.fft(E_in) * H)                     # ≈ dE/dt when pulse BW << linewidth
  • Heterodyne: the RF linewidth of free-running lasers is the sum of their linewidths (here 2 × 100 MHz); with phase locking it is limited by the reference.
  • Differentiator: the output has the two-lobe shape of |dE/dt|². The asymmetry and the residual offset of the second lobe come from the finite ring linewidth (pulse bandwidth not negligible compared with the resonance width). A narrower pulse would be even more distorted; a wider ring resonance is needed for faster signals.

Extra: optoelectronic oscillator (OEO)

Optional deep-dive.

Loop: laser → MZM → long delay (fibre or on-chip spiral/ring) → photodiode → RF amplifier → RF filter → back to the MZM electrode.

Oscillation conditions (Barkhausen):

  • loop gain \(G_{loop} \ge 1\)
  • loop phase \(= 2\pi m\) ⇒ modes spaced by \(1/\tau\)

Phase noise improves as the delay \(\tau\) grows (Yao & Maleki): long low-loss delays → very pure microwaves (< −140 dBc/Hz at 10 kHz offset with km of fibre).

Integrated version: the fibre is replaced by a high-Q ring, which acts both as delay (energy storage) and as the mode-selecting filter.

Why it belongs here: the OEO is a closed microwave photonic circuit. Its analysis uses exactly the blocks of this section:

  1. MZM transfer (\(J_1\) compression sets the amplitude)
  2. link gain \(G = (\pi I_{dc}/V_\pi)^2 R_S R_L\) ⇒ RF gain needed
  3. ring filter ⇒ mode selection
  4. shot/thermal/RIN noise ⇒ phase noise floor
  • Quick estimate: with G_link = −6 dB and 3 dB of filter loss, the RF amplifier must provide > 9 dB for oscillation to start.
  • The oscillation amplitude stabilises where the Bessel compression of the MZM brings the loop gain back to exactly 1.

RF Performance Metrics

System Characterization

Gain (G):

\(G = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_{RF,out}}{P_{RF,in}}\right) \text{ [dB]}\)

Noise Figure (NF):

\(NF = SNR_{in} - SNR_{out} \text{ [dB]}\)

Third-order intercept point (IP3) from one two-tone measurement:

\(IIP_3 = P_{in} + \frac{1}{2}\left(P_{out,fund} - P_{out,IM3}\right), \qquad OIP_3 = IIP_3 + G\)

Spurious-Free Dynamic Range:

\(SFDR = \frac{2}{3}(OIP_3 - N_{out}) = \frac{2}{3}(IIP_3 - N_{in,eq})\)

1-dB compression point (P1dB):

  • Input power where gain drops 1 dB
  • Indicates saturation
  • Typically ~10 dB below IP3
  • IIP3 from one measurement: the IM3 line rises 3 dB per dB, the fundamental 1 dB per dB; the gap closes at 2 dB per dB, hence the factor 1/2.
  • SFDR can be referred to output or input: same number, as long as the noise floor is referred to the same point (N_in,eq = N_out − G).
  • For an MZM at quadrature, P1dB is 9.6 dB below IIP3 (exact result of the J1 compression), close to the rule of thumb.

Performance of microwave photonics

  • Compare the integrated MWP demonstrations with discrete (fibre) systems: integrated links typically have higher NF because of on-chip losses and lower optical power handling.
  • The gap closes with low-V_π modulators (thin-film LiNbO₃) and high-power photodiodes.

Link Budget Analysis

System Design Methodology

Power budget (dB):

\(G_{total} = \sum_k G_k\) (optical losses count twice in RF dB)

Noise budget — Friis formula (linear units!):

\[F_{total} = F_1 + \frac{F_2 - 1}{G_1} + \frac{F_3 - 1}{G_1 G_2} + \cdots\]

Noise figures do not add in dB: the first stage dominates.

Example: photonic link alone: G = −6 dB, NF = 32 dB

Configuration NF total
link alone 32 dB
LNA (G = 20 dB, NF = 2 dB) + link 12.4 dB
link + post-amplifier ≈ 32 dB 
def friis(stages):                 # [(G_dB, NF_dB), ...]
    F, G = 0.0, 1.0
    for i, (g, nf) in enumerate(stages):
        f = 10**(nf/10)
        F += f if i == 0 else (f - 1)/G
        G *= 10**(g/10)
    return 10*np.log10(G), 10*np.log10(F)

print(friis([(-6, 32)]))            # (-6.0, 32.0)
print(friis([(20, 2), (-6, 32)]))   # (14.0, 12.4)

Design optimization:

  • Maximize optical power (until RIN-limited)
  • Minimize optical losses (×2 in RF dB)
  • Lower \(V_\pi\), match impedances
  • Pre-amplify before the link (but: the LNA reduces SFDR, it amplifies the interferers too)
  • F_total = 1.585 + (1585 − 1)/100 = 17.4 → 12.4 dB. The LNA’s 20 dB of gain divides the link’s noise contribution by 100.
  • Trade-off: the LNA raises all input signals by 20 dB, so the input IP3 of the cascade drops by about 20 dB; SFDR does not improve for free. Cascaded IP3 has its own formula (1/IIP3_tot = 1/IIP3_1 + G_1/IIP3_2 …).
  • The “optical loss counts twice” rule: 3 dB of coupling loss reduces I_dc by half and the RF gain by 6 dB.

class RingModel(i3.CompactModel):
    parameters = ["kappa", "length", "neff", "ng", "loss_dB_cm"]
    terms = [i3.OpticalTerm(name="in"), i3.OpticalTerm(name="out")]

    def calculate_smatrix(parameters, env, S):
        lam = env.wavelength
        neff = parameters.neff - (parameters.ng - parameters.neff) * (lam - 1.55) / 1.55
        a = 10 ** (-parameters.loss_dB_cm * parameters.length * 1e-4 / 20)
        t = (1 - parameters.kappa ** 2) ** 0.5
        e = a * np.exp(-1j * 2 * np.pi * neff * parameters.length / lam)
        S["in", "out"] = S["out", "in"] = (t - e) / (1 - t * e)

Waveguide Routing Best Practices

Minimizing Loss and Crosstalk

Bend radius selection: radiation loss grows exponentially as R decreases (next slide)

Practical: \(R \geq 5\ \mu m\) for 220 nm strip (1550 nm), ≥ 10–30 µm for rib waveguides; Euler (clothoid) bends reduce mode-mismatch loss at the straight–bend junction.

Crossing optimization:

  • Use expanded (MMI-based) crossings
  • Minimize number of crossings
  • 90° crossings preferred
  • Consider multi-layer routing (SiN over Si)

Typical crossing loss:

  • Simple crossing: 0.5–1 dB
  • Optimized crossing: < 0.1 dB, crosstalk < −35 dB

Crosstalk mitigation:

  • Spacing: ≥ 2 µm between strip waveguides for < −40 dB over mm lengths
  • Use substrate trenches
  • Shield sensitive paths
  • Group by function

Crosstalk levels:

  • Adjacent straight WG: <-40 dB
  • With crossings: <-30 dB
  • Curved sections: <-35 dB
  • Waveguide-to-waveguide crosstalk is a directional coupler with a large gap: the coupling length grows exponentially with the gap (Section A figure), so 2 µm spacing gives a cross-over length of many millimetres.
  • Long parallel runs are the risk: crosstalk accumulates with length; for long buses keep at least 3–5 µm or use wider waveguides.
  • Euler bends: the curvature increases linearly from zero, so the mode adapts adiabatically and the junction loss of a circular bend (mode mismatch) disappears.

Bend loss vs radius

Marcuse formula for a bent slab (applied to the EIM lateral profile):

\[\alpha_{bend} = C\, e^{-U R}, \qquad U = \frac{2\gamma^3}{3\beta^2}\]

\(\gamma\) = lateral decay constant of the mode in the cladding. Low lateral contrast (rib) ⇒ small \(\gamma\) ⇒ large radii.

def marcuse_bend(R, n1, n2, w, lam):
    k0 = 2*np.pi/lam
    beta = k0*slab_modes([n2, n1, n2], [w], lam)[0]
    kap = np.sqrt((n1*k0)**2 - beta**2)
    gam = np.sqrt(beta**2 - (n2*k0)**2)
    C = gam**2*kap**2*np.exp(gam*w) / \
        (beta*(1 + gam*w/2)*(kap**2 + gam**2))
    return C*np.exp(-2*gam**3/(3*beta**2)*R)
Waveguide R for 0.01 dB/90° (radiation only)
strip 500×220 ≈ 2.7 µm
rib, 90 nm slab ≈ 6 µm
rib, 150 nm slab ≈ 32 µm
  • The exponential dependence explains why the PDK minimum radius is a hard rule: going from 5 to 3 µm in a rib waveguide multiplies the loss by orders of magnitude.
  • Real minimum radii are larger than the radiation limit because of mode mismatch at the junction, sidewall scattering in the bend (the mode is pushed against the outer wall), and substrate leakage. PDKs quote measured values.
  • Rib waveguides are used for modulators (they need the slab for electrical contact), so the routing around modulators must transition to strip waveguides with a taper before bending tightly.

Thermal Management in Layout

Temperature Control Design

Heater placement:

  • Close to active region
  • Symmetric heating
  • Isolated thermal zones
  • Access to electrical pads

Thermal crosstalk:

\(\Delta T_{j} = \sum_i R_{th,ij}\, P_i\) (\(R_{th,ij}\) = mutual thermal resistance)

Mitigation:

  • Trenches between zones
  • Distance between heaters (> 20–50 μm)
  • Thermal simulation recommended
  • Calibrate the crosstalk matrix and invert it in the controller

Temperature monitoring:

  • Integrated sensors
  • Calibration structures
  • Feedback control capability

Heater design:

  • Serpentine patterns
  • TiN or doped Si
  • Typical resistance: \(100-1000\ \Omega\)
  • Power: 10-50 mW per device
  • Undercut (remove the substrate below): \(P_\pi\) ÷ 5–10, but slower (ms) and fragile
  • Thermal crosstalk is linear: the temperature of every waveguide is a matrix R_th times the vector of heater powers. Measuring this matrix and inverting it decouples the heaters in software.
  • Undercut trade-off: much lower P_π but time constant goes from µs to ms, and the suspended membrane must be mechanically designed.

Thermal crosstalk: 2D heat equation

Steady state, finite volumes on the SOI stack (Si substrate \(k\) = 130, oxide 1.4, air 0.026 W/m/K), heat sink at the bottom:

\[\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) = -q\]

# harmonic-mean conductance between neighbour cells
kh = 2*k[:, :-1]*k[:, 1:]/(k[:, :-1] + k[:, 1:])
# ... assemble sparse matrix A, heat source q
T = scipy.sparse.linalg.spsolve(A, q)

# P_pi of the heated waveguide:
dT_pi = lam/(2*L*dndT)        # 41.7 K for L = 100 um
P_pi  = dT_pi/dT_per_watt

Result: \(P_\pi\) ≈ 22 mW; crosstalk < 5% beyond ≈ 10 µm. Oxide trenches change little: the heat flows down into the substrate.

  • The 2D model assumes an infinitely long heater; real heaters spread heat also along their ends, so measured crosstalk is somewhat larger.
  • Counter-intuitive result for students: trenches through the oxide barely change P_π or crosstalk at large distance, because most heat goes vertically through 2 µm of oxide into the very conductive substrate. Trenches only help when combined with undercut, or for heaters very close to each other.
  • The ring heaters in a WDM demux are typically 30–50 µm apart: < 2% crosstalk, compensated by calibration.

Electrical Routing

RF and DC Distribution

DC routing:

  • Wide traces for current capacity
  • Star topology for sensitive bias
  • Separate analog/digital grounds
  • Decoupling capacitors

Trace width (IR drop):

\(W = \frac{I \cdot \rho \cdot L}{t \cdot \Delta V}\)

Typical: 2-10 μm for DC bias; also check electromigration (≈ 1–2 mA/µm for 1 µm thick Al)

RF routing:

  • Coplanar waveguide (CPW) preferred
  • 50 Ω characteristic impedance
  • Ground-signal-ground (GSG) pads
  • Minimize discontinuities

CPW dimensions:

\(Z_0 = \frac{30\pi}{\sqrt{\epsilon_{eff}}} \frac{K(k')}{K(k)}, \quad k = \frac{S}{S+2W}\)

Typical: 20 μm signal, 10 μm gaps

  • Example: a heater taking 30 mA through 2 mm of 1 µm thick Al (ρ = 2.8 × 10⁻⁸ Ωm) with 0.1 V allowed drop needs W ≈ 17 µm.
  • Electromigration often dominates the width choice for heaters, not IR drop.
  • CPW: K is the complete elliptic integral; for SiO₂ on Si ε_eff ≈ 6–7, and S/W ≈ 2 gives about 50 Ω. Keep the RF lines short and straight; every bend or width change is a reflection.

F. Yield Optimization

  • Section F was announced in the module structure: how process variations translate into device spread, how to estimate yield, and how to design so that a large fraction of chips meet the specification.
  • Running example: the resonance of a ring (or any interferometer) and its sensitivity to width and thickness.

Why yield is a photonics problem

Interferometric devices convert nanometres into nanometres:

\[\Delta\lambda_{res} = \frac{\lambda}{n_g}\,\Delta n_{eff} = \frac{\lambda}{n_g}\left(\frac{\partial n_{eff}}{\partial w}\Delta w + \frac{\partial n_{eff}}{\partial h}\Delta h + \frac{\partial n_{eff}}{\partial T}\Delta T\right)\]

Sensitivity (500×220 nm strip) Value
\(\partial\lambda/\partial w\) ≈ 1 nm/nm (FDE), 0.5 (EIM)
\(\partial\lambda/\partial h\) ≈ 2 nm/nm (FDE), 1.3 (EIM)
\(\partial\lambda/\partial T\) ≈ 0.08 nm/K

A 200 GHz WDM grid has 1.6 nm spacing: a few nm of width error moves a channel onto its neighbour.

Yield definition:

\[Y = P(\text{all specs met}) = \int_{\text{spec region}} p(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}\]

\(\mathbf{x}\) = process parameters (w, h, gap, doping …), \(p\) = their joint distribution.

Three estimation methods:

  1. Corner analysis — evaluate at ±3σ combinations (cheap, pessimistic, misses non-monotonic effects)
  2. Monte Carlo — sample \(p(\mathbf{x})\), count passes (general, needs fast models)
  3. Sensitivity (linear) analysis — \(\sigma_y^2 = \sum_i (\partial y/\partial x_i)^2 \sigma_i^2\) (instant, valid for small variations)
  • The thickness sensitivity is larger than the width sensitivity because the mode is more confined vertically (220 nm) than laterally (500 nm). Fortunately thickness varies slowly across the wafer (correlated) while width has both slow and local components.
  • The EIM numbers are about half of the full-vectorial values; the method is the same, only the model accuracy differs. Mention that a PDK gives these sensitivities directly or as corner models.

Sensitivity analysis in Python

from scipy.interpolate import RegularGridInterpolator
W = np.linspace(0.46, 0.54, 9);  H = np.linspace(0.205, 0.235, 7)
table = np.array([[neff_strip(w, h) for h in H] for w in W])
lut = RegularGridInterpolator((W, H), table)      # LUT compact model
n0, ng = lut([[0.5, 0.22]])[0], ng_strip(0.5)

shift = lambda w, h: 1550*(lut(np.c_[w, h]) - n0)/ng   # nm

d = 1e-3                                           # 1 nm finite difference
s_w = (shift([0.5+d], [0.22]) - shift([0.5-d], [0.22]))[0]/2
s_h = (shift([0.5], [0.22+d]) - shift([0.5], [0.22-d]))[0]/2
sigma = np.hypot(s_w*5, s_h*2)                     # σw = 5 nm, σh = 2 nm
print(s_w, s_h, sigma)                             # 0.50, 1.32, ≈ 3.6 nm

Reading the result:

  • Linear estimate σ ≈ 3.6 nm agrees with Monte Carlo (next slide): the model is linear over ±3σ.
  • Thickness dominates the budget even with σ_h < σ_w.
  • Design levers to reduce sensitivity:
    • wider waveguides (∂n/∂w falls as w grows)
    • multimode (2–3 µm) waveguides in long delay lines
    • adiabatic/broadband couplers instead of DCs
    • differential designs: devices that see the same error cancel it (MZI arms, ring pairs)
  • This slide is the “derivative” version of yield: fast enough to run inside an optimisation loop.
  • Ask: what width would make ∂λ/∂w ten times smaller? (Look at the n_eff vs width figure: around 1–2 µm the curve flattens; that is why low-sensitivity designs use wide waveguides.)
  • Differential design: in an unbalanced MZI, a uniform width error shifts both arms; only the difference ΔL matters for the FSR, though the absolute fringe position still moves.

Monte Carlo, corners and tuning budget

w = 0.5 + rng.normal(0, 0.005, N); h = 0.22 + rng.normal(0, 0.002, N)
d = shift(w, h)                                   # nm
need = -(d + offset)                              # red shift the heater must supply
yield_ = np.mean((need >= 0) & (need <= tuning_range))
  • Corners at ±3σ in both variables give ±15 nm: far beyond the real spread (±11 nm at 3σ of the joint distribution). Corners are pessimistic.
  • Heaters only red-shift: centring the design on the blue side (−3 to −6 nm) is worth more than adding heater power.
  • Corners treat w and h as simultaneously at +3σ, a 0.3%×0.3% event; the joint distribution has a 3σ ellipse that is much smaller. Corner design wastes margin (over-design) in this case.
  • Right panel: with no offset, half of the devices are already “too red” and no heater can fix them: yield saturates at 50%. With a −6 nm offset almost every device can be tuned, but you need about 12 nm (≈ 150 K!) of tuning range. Real systems combine a blue offset, a modest heater range, and channel re-assignment (each ring locks to the nearest channel: “FSR-wrapping”).
  • Power cost: 0.08 nm/K and ≈ 0.25 nm/mW for an efficient ring heater → 6 nm costs ≈ 25 mW per ring.

  • Systematic part (radial, gradient) dominates: dies fail at the edge, not randomly.
  • Within one die, devices are correlated: neighbouring rings shift together ⇒ channel spacing is preserved.
  • Use: wafer-level test maps → die binning, model re-centring, and feedback to the fab.
  • The systematic part can be measured and compensated (e.g. per-die heater offset stored in the transceiver firmware); the random part is what really costs yield.
  • Real wafer maps of 220 nm SOI show a few nm of thickness variation centre-to-edge; resonance maps of ring arrays look very similar to this simulation.

Design for yield: strategies

Reduce sensitivity (design)

  • Wide / multimode waveguides where possible
  • Broadband components (MMI, adiabatic couplers, Euler bends)
  • Athermal designs (negative dn/dT cladding, MZI compensation)
  • Tolerant coupling (gap sweep chosen at a flat region)

Centre the design (statistics)

  • Design on the distribution, not on the nominal point
  • Offset resonances to the blue (heaters only red-shift)
  • Choose the gap/width that maximises yield, not peak performance

Compensate (system)

  • Heaters + control loops (wavelength locking)
  • Trimming: post-fabrication (e-beam/laser annealing, PCM, implant-based)
  • Calibration tables stored per chip
  • Redundancy: spare rings, channel re-mapping

Measure (test)

  • Test structures on every die (loss, ring, coupler gap series)
  • Wafer-level optical testing → binning
  • Feed measurements back into the PDK statistical models
  • The four columns are in order of cost: design choices are free, centring is free, compensation costs power and control electronics, testing costs time.
  • Example of centring: a 2nd-order filter has a flat top; choosing slightly over-coupled rings widens the passband and increases yield at a small cost in insertion loss.
  • Trimming technologies (Module 5: PCMs) are an active research topic precisely because of this yield problem.

Extra: system yield and test economics

Optional deep-dive.

System yield is a product:

\[Y_{chip} = \prod_{k=1}^{N} Y_k \quad (\text{independent failures})\]

  • 16 rings with 99% yield each: \(Y = 0.99^{16} = 85\%\)
  • 64 rings: 53% ⇒ per-device yield must be ≥ 99.9% for large circuits

Correlated variations help: if all rings shift together, one global correction fixes all ⇒ the system yield is closer to the single-device yield.

Cost per good die:

\[C_{good} = \frac{C_{wafer}/N_{die} + C_{test} + C_{package}}{Y_{wafer}\cdot Y_{test}\cdot Y_{package}}\]

  • Packaging (fibre attach) is often > 50% of the cost of a photonic module
  • ⇒ test before packaging: wafer-level optical probing removes bad dies before the expensive step
  • Known-good-die strategy: the same logic as for chiplets in electronics
  • Compute with students: wafer 5000 €, 200 dies, packaging 100 €, packaging yield 90%, wafer yield 70%. Without wafer test, bad dies get packaged: (25 + 100)/(0.7 × 0.9) ≈ 198 € per good module. With wafer test (5 € per die): (25 + 5)/0.7 + 100/0.9 ≈ 154 €.
  • This is the economic reason why grating couplers (testable on wafer) remain popular even though edge couplers have lower loss.

Key Takeaways

  1. Compact models reduce each component to an S-matrix depending on λ, V, T and geometry; build them from physics simulation, validate them on silicon, and check reciprocity, passivity and causality.
  2. Circuits are solved by eliminating internal ports: \(S_{ext} = S_{ee} + S_{ei}(I - CS_{ii})^{-1}CS_{ie}\) — the core of every photonic circuit simulator.
  3. A PDK is the contract between foundry and designer: layers, rules (DRC/LVS), cells/PCells, models and their statistics.
  4. Analog photonic links: \(G = (\pi I_{dc}/V_\pi)^2R_SR_L\); NF is thermal → shot → RIN limited; SFDR comes from the IM3 slope of 3. Optical loss counts twice in RF dB; use Friis for cascades.
  5. MWP functions (filters, TTD, combs, mixing, differentiation) exploit the huge optical bandwidth and wavelength-independent delays.
  6. Layout turns the circuit into geometry: bends (exponential loss), crossings, thermal crosstalk (substrate-dominated), RF lines, pads and fill.
  7. Yield: interferometric devices shift by ~1–2 nm per nm of geometry error; design on the distribution (Monte Carlo), centre to the blue, and budget tuning power.
  • Ask students to give one number for each takeaway: e.g. the FSR of a 50 µm MZI, the link gain at 10 mA, the minimum strip bend radius, the resonance shift for 5 nm of width.
